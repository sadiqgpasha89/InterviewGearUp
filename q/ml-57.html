What role do Cross-Encoder Re-Ranking models play in refining candidate passages retrieved by bi-encoders?

ML / AI engineer · Gear 3 (Senior) · RAG · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

A bi-encoder embeds query and document separately, so documents are indexed offline and retrieval is fast but approximate. A cross-encoder feeds the query and a passage together into one transformer, letting every query token attend to every passage token for a much more accurate relevance score, but it must run per pair, so it is applied only to the top candidates as a reranker.

Explained in depth

Two-stage retrieval

  1. Stage 1: candidate generation (recall-oriented): BM25 and/or bi-encoder dense retrieval returns the top 50 to 200 passages from millions or billions in milliseconds, using precomputed document vectors and ANN indexes.
  2. Stage 2: reranking (precision-oriented): a more expensive model re-scores only those candidates and reorders them; the top 3 to 10 go to the LLM.

Bi-encoder limits

In a bi-encoder, sim(q, d) = f(q) · g(d): the query and document are encoded independently into single vectors, so fine-grained interactions are lost (negation, exact constraints, "which of these two entities does the sentence say is larger"). Compressing a passage into one vector discards detail. This design is what enables caching and ANN search.

Cross-encoder

Input is the concatenation [CLS] query [SEP] passage [SEP], passed through the full transformer so self-attention operates jointly across query and passage tokens. A linear head on [CLS] outputs a relevance score (or logit).

from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder
reranker = CrossEncoder("BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3", max_length=512)
pairs = [(query, p.text) for p in candidates]
scores = reranker.predict(pairs, batch_size=32)
ranked = [p for _, p in sorted(zip(scores, candidates), key=lambda x: -x[0])][:5]

Models are trained on relevance-labelled pairs (MS MARCO) with hard negatives (passages the first-stage retriever ranked high but are wrong), using pointwise, pairwise or listwise loss. Examples: bge-reranker, Cohere Rerank, ms-marco-MiniLM cross-encoders, Jina reranker, mxbai-rerank, and fine-tuned domain rerankers.

Why not use it for everything

Cost scales as O(number of documents × transformer cost) per query: scoring 10 million passages would take hours. Therefore it only runs on the top-k candidates (typically 20 to 100), costing tens to hundreds of milliseconds on GPU (more on CPU).

Benefits

  • Large gains in precision@k, nDCG and MRR (often 5 to 20 percent over bi-encoder alone), and better answers because the LLM receives fewer, more relevant passages (less noise, lower token cost, less "lost in the middle").
  • Corrects failures of semantic retrieval (similar but wrong passages) and complements BM25 recall.
  • Lets you use a cheap fast retriever tuned for recall.

Alternatives and variants

  • Late-interaction (ColBERT): per-token embeddings and MaxSim scoring; near cross-encoder quality with precomputed document token vectors, but bigger index.
  • LLM rerankers (listwise prompts, RankGPT, RankZephyr): flexible and instruction-following, slower and costlier.
  • Learned score thresholds to drop irrelevant candidates and allow "no answer".
  • Distilled small rerankers for latency budgets, and quantised/ONNX/TensorRT serving.

Operational guidance

Pick candidate count by measuring recall@k of stage 1 (rerank cannot recover documents not retrieved), batch requests on GPU, cap passage length (512 tokens), cache scores for repeated queries, monitor latency percentiles, and evaluate on a labelled set both with and without reranking. Fine-tune on in-domain click or judgement data when generic rerankers underperform.

Pitfalls

Reranking too few candidates (low ceiling), too many (latency), truncating passages so the evidence is cut, comparing scores across different queries as absolute, and ignoring licensing for commercial rerankers.
