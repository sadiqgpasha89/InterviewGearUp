What causes hallucinations in generative LLMs and what architectural mitigations reduce their frequency?

ML / AI engineer · Gear 3 (Senior) · LLMs · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

LLMs hallucinate because they are trained to predict plausible next tokens, not verify truth. Causes include gaps and errors in training data, no grounding in sources, uncertain long-tail facts, fluent-biased decoding, and rewards that discourage saying I do not know. Mitigations combine retrieval grounding, better training and calibration, constrained decoding, verification and guardrails.

Explained in depth

What hallucination is

A hallucination is generated content that is fluent but false, unsupported or fabricated: invented citations, wrong dates, nonexistent API functions, fake legal cases.

  • Intrinsic hallucination contradicts the provided source or prompt (summary says the opposite of the document).
  • Extrinsic hallucination adds claims that cannot be verified from the source (or the world).
  • Factuality versus faithfulness: correctness with respect to world knowledge versus fidelity to the given context.

Why it happens

  1. Training objective: next-token prediction maximises likelihood of plausible text, not truth. The model learns a statistical distribution; fluent fabrications have high probability when facts are uncertain.
  2. Data issues: noisy, outdated or contradictory web data; long-tail facts seen rarely (the model memorises poorly and guesses); biases; duplication.
  3. Knowledge boundaries: parametric memory is lossy and fixed at the cutoff; the model does not know what it does not know and is overconfident (poor calibration after RLHF).
  4. Alignment pressure: reward models and human raters may favour confident, complete answers over abstention, sycophancy (agreeing with false premises).
  5. Decoding: sampling with high temperature increases errors; greedy decoding can still produce confident mistakes; exposure bias compounds errors over long outputs (snowballing).
  6. Context problems: long prompts with the answer buried ("lost in the middle"), conflicting retrieved passages, ambiguous or underspecified questions, tool outputs ignored.
  7. Reasoning limits: multi-step reasoning errors, arithmetic, and compositional generalisation failures.

Mitigations (architecture and system level)

  • Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): ground answers in retrieved documents from trusted corpora; instruct the model to answer only from context, cite passages, and say "not found". Improve retrieval quality (hybrid search, reranking, chunking). RAG still hallucinates when retrieval fails or the model ignores context, so evaluate faithfulness.
  • Tool use: calculators, code execution, search, databases and APIs for facts and computation instead of recall.
  • Better training: high-quality data curation and deduplication, factuality-focused fine-tuning, RLHF or DPO with truthfulness rewards, training to abstain and express uncertainty, knowledge editing, domain fine-tuning, and process supervision for reasoning.
  • Prompting and decoding: lower temperature, clear instructions, step-by-step reasoning (chain-of-thought) with verification, self-consistency (sample multiple answers, take the majority), chain-of-verification (generate verification questions and answer them independently), constrained or structured decoding (JSON schemas, grammar), contrastive decoding (DoLa).
  • Verification and post-processing: NLI or entailment checks of claims against sources, citation verification, fact-checking with retrieval, LLM-as-judge, SelfCheckGPT (consistency across samples), confidence thresholds using token log-probs or semantic entropy, and fallback to "I do not know" or human escalation.
  • Product design: show sources, highlight uncertainty, restrict scope, keep humans in the loop for high-stakes outputs, log and review failures.
  • Guardrails: policy checks on inputs and outputs (see guardrail frameworks).

Evaluation

TruthfulQA, HaluEval, FActScore, RAGAS faithfulness, domain-specific test sets with known answers; measure hallucination rate, abstention accuracy, and citation precision.

Realistic expectations

Hallucination can be reduced, not eliminated, with current architectures; defence in depth (grounding, verification, abstention, monitoring) is the standard approach.

Pitfalls

Assuming RAG removes hallucination, trusting model-stated confidence, evaluating only on easy questions, and optimising for fluency or user satisfaction scores that reward confident wrong answers.

Likely follow-ups

  • What is the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic hallucination?
  • How does retrieval-augmented generation reduce but not eliminate hallucination?
  • How can uncertainty estimation or self-consistency detect hallucinated claims?
