How does the Two-Phase Commit (2PC) protocol work, and why does it fail under coordinator partitions?

Backend engineer · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Distributed systems · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Two-phase commit has a coordinator ask every participant to prepare (durably promise they can commit) and then, if all say yes, tell them all to commit, otherwise abort. It is a blocking protocol: if the coordinator fails or is partitioned after participants voted yes, they are stuck in doubt, holding locks, because they cannot safely commit or abort alone.

Explained in depth

The protocol

  1. Prepare (voting): the coordinator sends PREPARE. Each participant does the work, writes the transaction to its log in a prepared state, keeps its locks, and votes yes or no.
  2. Commit or abort: if every vote is yes, the coordinator logs the decision to commit and sends COMMIT; if any vote is no or times out, it sends ABORT. Participants apply the decision and release locks.

Once a participant has voted yes, it has given up the right to abort on its own.

Why it blocks

If the coordinator crashes after collecting yes votes (or the network splits it from a participant), prepared participants do not know the outcome. Committing might contradict a decision to abort, and aborting might contradict a decision to commit. They must wait, holding locks and blocking other transactions, until the coordinator recovers (its decision is in its log) or an operator intervenes. A participant that has not yet voted can safely abort on timeout.

Costs

Two network round trips and several forced log writes per transaction, locks held across the whole protocol, and availability tied to the slowest or least reliable participant.

Alternatives and mitigations

  • Replicate the coordinator with consensus (Paxos or Raft), as Spanner does with 2PC across Paxos groups, so there is no single point of failure.
  • Three-phase commit reduces blocking but is not safe under network partitions, so it is rarely used.
  • Sagas with compensating actions, and the outbox pattern, avoid cross-service transactions in microservices, at the price of weaker isolation.
  • XA is the standard interface for 2PC between databases and message brokers.
