Explain the NIST Incident Response lifecycle: Preparation, Detection, Containment, Eradication, and Recovery.

Security engineer · Gear 3 (Senior) · Operations · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

The NIST incident response lifecycle (SP 800-61) has four phases: Preparation, Detection and Analysis, Containment, Eradication and Recovery, and Post-Incident Activity. It is cyclical: readiness, plans and tooling come first, then identifying and scoping an incident, stopping its spread, removing the cause and restoring service, and finally learning lessons that feed back into preparation.

Explained in depth

Framework

NIST SP 800-61 (Revision 2; Revision 3 aligns the lifecycle with NIST CSF 2.0 functions) describes four phases. Many teams cite the SANS six-step version (Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, Lessons Learned), which is the same activities split more finely: Preparation, Detection and Analysis, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, Post-Incident Activity.

1. Preparation

Most incident outcomes are decided before the incident.

  • Policy and plan: incident response policy, roles (incident commander, communications, legal, HR, forensics, IT), severity definitions, escalation paths, contact lists and out-of-hours on-call, decision authority (who may shut down production), regulatory notification timelines (GDPR 72 hours, state breach laws, SEC rules, PCI, sector regulators).
  • Playbooks for likely scenarios (ransomware, phishing and business email compromise, data exfiltration, insider, DDoS, cloud credential leak, web application compromise, lost device).
  • Tooling and access: SIEM, EDR, central logging with retention, forensic workstations and imaging tools, jump kits, clean-room communications (out-of-band channel if email is compromised), ticketing, evidence storage with chain of custody, asset and data inventory, network diagrams, backups tested for restore, contracts with IR retainer, legal counsel and cyber insurers.
  • Hardening and training: patching, MFA, least privilege, segmentation, awareness, tabletop exercises and simulations at least annually, purple team tests.

2. Detection and Analysis

  • Sources: alerts (SIEM, EDR, IDS), user reports, threat intelligence, third-party notifications (law enforcement, researchers), anomalies.
  • Validate and triage: is it a true incident? Determine scope, impact, affected assets and data, attack vector, attacker actions and timeline; classify severity and category; document everything (timestamps, who did what, evidence).
  • Analysis: correlate logs, examine endpoints and network captures, use indicators of compromise and MITRE ATT&CK, determine whether data was accessed or exfiltrated, identify patient zero.
  • Declare the incident, assign an incident commander, notify stakeholders, start legal and regulatory clocks, begin evidence preservation (volatile data first: memory, running processes, network connections).

3. Containment

Limit damage and prevent spread while keeping evidence.

  • Short-term containment (immediate): isolate affected hosts via EDR network quarantine, disable compromised accounts, revoke tokens and sessions, block malicious IPs and domains, take compromised cloud keys out of service, segment network, take affected application out of rotation.
  • Long-term containment: temporary fixes that let business continue while eradicating (rebuild clean systems in an isolated VLAN, apply interim compensating controls, password resets).
  • Decision factors: potential damage, need to preserve evidence, service availability, time and resources, effectiveness, duration. Avoid tipping off a sophisticated attacker before you understand scope (coordinated containment).
  • Take forensic images and memory dumps before reimaging; maintain chain of custody.

4. Eradication

Remove the root cause: delete malware and persistence (scheduled tasks, run keys, web shells, rogue accounts, OAuth app grants), close the exploited vulnerability (patch, configuration change), reset all potentially exposed credentials and keys, rebuild compromised hosts from known-good images, scan for other affected systems using IOCs, hunt for attacker footholds.

5. Recovery

Restore systems to normal operation safely: restore from verified clean backups, rebuild, bring services back in phases with enhanced monitoring, validate integrity and functionality, confirm no reinfection, monitor closely for recurrence (heightened logging for weeks), and coordinate communications to customers and regulators.

6. Post-incident activity

  • Lessons-learned review (blameless, within days to two weeks): timeline, what worked, detection gaps, response delays, root cause, decisions, communication issues.
  • Actions with owners and deadlines: new detections, hardening, policy changes, training, tooling, updates to playbooks and risk register.
  • Reporting: executive summary, regulatory notifications, insurance claims, customer communications, evidence retention for legal proceedings, metrics (time to detect, contain and recover; dwell time; cost).
  • Feed insights back into preparation, closing the loop.

Practical points

Keep a single incident log; separate roles of investigation lead and incident commander; use a war room with defined communication cadence; limit information to need-to-know; do not reveal response plans over compromised channels; involve legal early for privilege and disclosure obligations; do not "hack back"; assume the attacker may monitor email.

Common mistakes

Rebuilding systems before collecting evidence, rushing eradication without scoping (attackers return), unclear authority, untested backups, skipping the post-incident review, and treating response as an IT-only problem.
