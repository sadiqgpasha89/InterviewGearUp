What is difference-in-differences, what assumptions does it need, and how do you validate them?

Data scientist · Gear 3 (Senior) · Causal inference · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Difference-in-differences compares the before-after change in a treated group with the before-after change in a comparison group, which removes fixed group differences and common time shocks. It relies on parallel trends, no anticipation and stable composition, which you check with pre-period trends, event-study plots and placebo tests.

Explained in depth

When to use it

You cannot randomise (a price change in one city, a policy rolled out in some states, a feature launched to one market), but you have outcomes before and after for a treated group and a comparison group. DiD estimates the treatment effect by differencing out stable differences between groups and shocks affecting both.

The estimator

With group means: DiD = (Y_treated,post - Y_treated,pre) - (Y_control,post - Y_control,pre). Example: sales in test stores rise from 100 to 130 (+30) while control stores rise from 90 to 105 (+15); the estimated effect is +15. Regression form (two-way fixed effects for the simple 2 x 2 case): Y_it = a + b1 Treated_i + b2 Post_t + b3 (Treated_i x Post_t) + e_it, where b3 is the DiD estimate. Add unit and time fixed effects, covariates, and cluster standard errors by unit (or by the level of treatment assignment).

import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
m = smf.ols("y ~ treated * post", data=df).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["unit"]})
print(m.params["treated:post"], m.conf_int().loc["treated:post"].values)

Key assumptions

  1. Parallel trends: without treatment, the treated group's outcome would have moved in parallel with the control's. Untestable after treatment, but testable in the pre-period.
  2. No anticipation: units do not change behaviour before the treatment starts.
  3. Stable composition and no spillovers (SUTVA): the same kinds of units appear before and after; the control is not affected by treatment (a promotion in one city pulling customers from the control city breaks this).
  4. No other simultaneous shocks hitting only one group.

Validation

  • Plot pre-period trends for both groups over many periods.
  • Event-study specification: estimate effects for each period relative to treatment (leads and lags). Leads should be near zero; a trend in leads is a warning.
  • Placebo tests: fake treatment dates in the pre-period, or a placebo outcome not expected to change, or placebo groups.
  • Robustness to the choice of control group (matching or weighting controls on pre-trends), to time windows, and to excluding outlier units.
  • Look at levels as well as logs (parallel trends in logs does not imply parallel in levels).

Modern issues

  • Staggered adoption with heterogeneous effects: classic two-way fixed effects can give biased, even wrongly signed estimates because already-treated units serve as controls for later-treated ones. Use estimators designed for it (Callaway and Sant'Anna, Sun and Abraham, de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfoeuille, Borusyak et al. imputation) and report event-study plots.
  • Few treated clusters (one city): standard errors are unreliable; use wild cluster bootstrap, randomisation inference, or synthetic control (build a weighted combination of control units matching the treated unit's pre-trend).
  • Conditional parallel trends: when groups differ in trends driven by covariates, adjust with covariates or doubly robust DiD.
  • Continuous or varying intensity treatments need dose-response versions.

Reporting

State the estimand (ATT), show raw trends, event-study estimates, confidence intervals, robustness checks, and the plausibility argument for parallel trends; convert to business impact.

Pitfalls

Choosing a control group because it was convenient, ignoring pre-trends, using plain TWFE with staggered rollout, and reporting naive standard errors that ignore serial correlation.
