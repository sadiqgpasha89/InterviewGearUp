How do you solicit and act on upward feedback from your direct reports to improve your management style?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Management · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Ask for upward feedback in a safe and structured way: set the expectation, use one-to-ones and anonymous surveys, ask specific questions, listen without defending, thank the person, summarise what you heard, share the one or two changes you will make, follow up visibly, and ask again. Acting on feedback is what makes people keep giving it.

Explained in depth

Why it matters

Managers have blind spots, and their style heavily influences engagement, retention and performance. Direct reports see how you actually behave daily, but the power imbalance makes honest feedback risky for them. Seeking feedback, acting on it and modelling vulnerability builds trust and psychological safety, and shows that growth applies to everyone.

Create the conditions

  1. Say it explicitly and early: "I want to be a better manager, and I need your input. I will not hold anything you say against you." Explain how feedback will be used. Repeat regularly.
  2. Model receptiveness: share your own development goals, a mistake you made and what you learned, and publicly thank people for challenging you. Reward candour; never retaliate, even subtly.
  3. Choose multiple channels, because people differ in comfort:
  • One-to-one questions (regularly, not just annually).
  • Anonymous surveys (Culture Amp, Lattice, Google Forms, Officevibe) with manager-effectiveness items, run twice a year with enough respondents to preserve anonymity.
  • Skip-level conversations by your own manager or HR, who aggregate themes.
  • Team retrospectives that include "how can I support you better?"
  • 360 review with peers and stakeholders.
  • A third party or coach to interview the team and summarise themes.

Ask good questions

Avoid "Do you have any feedback for me?" (people say no). Ask specific, easy-to-answer and forward-looking questions:

  • "What is one thing I could start, stop or continue doing that would make your work easier?"
  • "Where do I get in your way? Where do you want more or less direction from me?"
  • "How could I give you better feedback or support your growth?"
  • "When have I helped you most? When have I let you down?"
  • "On a scale of 1 to 10, how well do my one-to-ones work for you? What would make them a 9?"
  • "Is there anything about how I run meetings or make decisions that is unclear?"

Use a shared document of management preferences ("how to work with me") and ask people to add theirs. Leave silence, and follow up if they hesitate; sometimes feedback comes in the next conversation.

Receive it well

  • Listen to understand: stay calm, ask clarifying questions ("Can you give me an example? What would you have preferred?"), and paraphrase back. Resist defending, explaining or arguing. A simple "Thank you, that is helpful. I want to think about it" is enough in the moment.
  • Notice your reactions (body language, tone), because defensiveness teaches people not to speak up again.
  • Look for patterns across sources rather than reacting to one comment; discuss with a mentor or your manager to calibrate. Not all feedback is right, but all of it contains information about perception.

Act and close the loop

  1. Summarise themes and choose one or two concrete changes you will make (for example "I will give feedback within 48 hours", "I will stop adding urgent tasks via DM on Fridays"). Publicly share them with the team ("Here is what I heard, here is what I will work on, and here is what I will not change and why"). Explaining why you will not act on something is part of respect.
  2. Follow through with visible behaviour change and ask for check-ins ("Have you noticed any difference?") at 30 to 60 days. Make your development goals part of your own performance plan.
  3. Repeat regularly, and show results. Acting on feedback increases the amount and quality you receive.
  4. Protect confidentiality, do not try to identify anonymous authors, and thank the whole team.

Measure

Manager effectiveness scores trend, team engagement, the number of improvements implemented from feedback, retention, and quality of one-to-ones.

Pitfalls

Asking once a year and doing nothing, punishing or sulking after critical feedback, defending your intentions, collecting data but not sharing actions, and ignoring cultural differences in how people give feedback.

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you get honest feedback from direct reports when there is a power imbalance and they fear repercussions?
  • How do you respond in the moment when feedback is critical or feels unfair?
  • How do you decide which feedback to act on and communicate that back to the team?
