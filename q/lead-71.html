How do you identify and address imposter syndrome among high-potential junior and mid-level engineers?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · People · Difficulty: easy · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Imposter syndrome is a persistent feeling of being a fraud despite evidence of competence. Address it by normalising it, giving specific evidence-based praise, setting clear expectations and growth paths, pairing people with mentors, encouraging learning from mistakes, assigning stretch work with support, and creating psychological safety so people ask questions without fear.

Explained in depth

What it looks like

Engineers with imposter feelings attribute success to luck or help, discount achievements, fear being "found out", avoid asking questions, overprepare, refuse stretch assignments or promotion, compare themselves with others, or become perfectionists. Common among high-potential juniors and mid-level engineers, people from underrepresented groups, career changers, and those entering a larger or more senior environment. It is not a diagnosis and not the same as genuine skill gaps; the leader's job is to distinguish them and to create conditions where capable people can see their capability.

Spot the signals

Reluctance to speak in meetings, constant apologising and hedging ("this may be a dumb question"), declining ownership, excessive time on small tasks, avoiding code reviews or demos, taking little credit, burnout, or comments like "I do not belong here". Create regular one-to-ones where they can talk, and observe patterns such as drops in participation after criticism.

What helps

  1. Normalise and open the conversation: share your own experiences, tell stories of senior engineers who have struggled, and note that many accomplished people feel this way. Do it privately and without labelling the person. Ask: "What worries you most right now? What would make you feel more confident?"
  2. Psychological safety: reward questions, say "I do not know" as a leader, run blameless postmortems, avoid sarcasm and public criticism, and make it routine to ask for help. Make onboarding, code review and meetings welcoming spaces.
  3. Clear expectations and visible growth: give a career ladder and written expectations for their level, define what "good" looks like, set 30-60-90 goals, and give regular progress check-ins. Uncertainty feeds self-doubt; clarity reduces it.
  4. Specific, evidence-based feedback: describe concrete achievements and their impact ("Your fix reduced checkout errors by 18 percent, and your explanation in the review helped two engineers"), not vague praise ("good job"). Keep a "wins" or brag document and encourage them to maintain it; revisit it before performance reviews. Also provide honest constructive feedback with a growth framing ("yet"), so praise is credible.
  5. Stretch assignments with scaffolding: give slightly harder tasks with a mentor, design review, pairing, and checkpoints. Success builds self-efficacy more than reassurance. Break larger goals into milestones.
  6. Mentoring and community: pair with a supportive senior mentor, and connect to peers (cohorts, guilds, employee resource groups, communities). Encourage them to mentor others or present a short talk, which reinforces expertise.
  7. Reframe mistakes: treat errors as learning data, share a team "failure wall" or "what we learned" sessions, and respond calmly to incidents. Separate the person from the outcome.
  8. Credit and visibility: attribute work publicly, include them in demos and meetings where decisions occur, and sponsor them for opportunities. Beware of overlooking quiet contributors.
  9. Review processes with fairness: ensure promotion and performance criteria are explicit and unbiased, check for gender, racial and tenure patterns in feedback, and avoid vague "executive presence" criteria.
  10. Wellbeing: watch for anxiety, burnout or depression; provide access to employee assistance programmes and encourage healthy boundaries. Suggest professional support when appropriate without diagnosing.

Balance support and accountability

Hold standards: set clear goals, provide feedback on gaps, and avoid lowering expectations out of sympathy. Express confidence ("I assigned this because I believe you can do it, and I will support you"). Review actual outcomes regularly and compare perception with evidence.

Measure

Engagement and belonging surveys, participation rates, promotion rates of mentored groups, retention, and self-reported confidence.

Pitfalls

Dismissing feelings ("you are great, stop worrying"), praising generically, avoiding giving critical feedback, overprotecting from challenge, and assuming it is only a personal issue when team environment contributes.
