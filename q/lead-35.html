How do you design a fair, sustainable on-call rotation that prevents alert fatigue and sleep disruption?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Operations · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

A sustainable on-call rotation shares load fairly across enough people, keeps shifts to about a week with a primary and secondary, protects sleep with follow-the-sun coverage or compensation for night work, and reduces pages by tuning alerts so they are urgent, actionable and symptom-based. Track page volume and toil, and invest in fixing the sources of noise.

Explained in depth

Rotation design

  • Team size: at least 6 to 8 people for a one-in-six or one-in-eight cadence. Smaller teams burn out; combine teams or add a shared rotation.
  • Primary and secondary on-call, with a clear escalation path and a manager or incident commander for major incidents.
  • Shift length: usually one week; handovers with a short note of open issues.
  • Follow-the-sun across time zones for 24 by 7 services, so nobody is woken regularly.
  • Fairness: swap rules, holiday coverage shared, no repeated unpopular slots, and compensation: pay, time off in lieu or both, especially for off-hours pages.
  • Onboarding: shadowing, runbooks, and a simple "reverse shadow" before going solo.

Preventing alert fatigue

  • Page only on urgent, actionable, user-impacting conditions (symptoms such as SLO burn), not on causes or every metric spike. Everything else goes to a ticket or a daily review.
  • Every alert has an owner, a runbook, and a clear severity.
  • Review alerts after each shift: delete, tune or automate noisy ones. Track pages per shift, the share that were actionable, and night pages; a common target is no more than a few pages per shift.
  • Deduplicate and group alerts, and add maintenance windows.

Sustainability

  • After a hard night, allow rest the next day without penalty.
  • Limit toil (manual, repetitive work) to under about 50 percent of the on-call engineer's time and reserve engineering time to fix recurring issues.
  • Follow up on every significant page with a fix, and use postmortems for lessons.
  • Ask the team regularly how on-call feels, and treat unhealthy load as a priority.
