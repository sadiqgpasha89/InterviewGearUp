Explain Bayesian inference in practice: priors, posteriors, MCMC sampling and how to check a model.

Data scientist · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Bayesian statistics · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Bayesian inference combines a prior over parameters with the likelihood of the data to obtain a posterior distribution, usually sampled with MCMC methods such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Choose weakly informative priors, check convergence with R-hat, effective sample size and divergences, and validate with posterior predictive checks and sensitivity to the prior before using the posterior for decisions.

Explained in depth

The framework

Bayes' rule: posterior is proportional to likelihood x prior, p(theta | data) ∝ p(data | theta) p(theta). The prior encodes what we know before seeing data, the likelihood is the data-generating model, and the posterior is the updated belief expressed as a full distribution. Predictions integrate over parameter uncertainty (the posterior predictive distribution). Credible intervals have the direct interpretation "given the model and prior, there is a 95 percent probability the parameter lies in this range". When the prior is conjugate (Beta-Binomial, Normal-Normal, Gamma-Poisson) the posterior is analytic; for realistic models we sample.

Priors

  • Weakly informative priors regularise without dominating the data (Normal(0, 2.5) on standardised logistic coefficients; half-Normal or exponential for scale parameters).
  • Informative priors encode past experiments or domain knowledge (previous A/B test lifts are usually between -2 and +5 percent).
  • Avoid flat "uninformative" priors on unbounded parameters, which can be improper or put mass on absurd values.
  • Do prior predictive checks: simulate data from the prior alone and see if they look plausible.
  • Run sensitivity analysis: refit with alternative priors; if conclusions change, say so and collect more data.

Computation

MCMC draws samples from the posterior. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo / NUTS (Stan, PyMC, NumPyro) uses gradients to explore efficiently in high dimensions. Alternatives: variational inference (fast approximations; can underestimate uncertainty), Laplace approximation, and importance sampling or SMC.

import pymc as pm, arviz as az
with pm.Model() as m:
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", 0, 1)
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", 1)
    y = pm.Normal("y", mu, sigma, observed=data)
    idata = pm.sample(2000, tune=1000, chains=4, target_accept=0.9)
print(az.summary(idata, var_names=["mu", "sigma"]))       # r_hat, ess_bulk, hdi

Diagnostics before trusting results

  • R-hat close to 1.00 (below 1.01): chains agree.
  • Effective sample size (bulk and tail) large enough (hundreds per parameter).
  • Trace plots well-mixed; no divergent transitions (sign of difficult geometry: reparameterise, use non-centred form, tighten priors, raise target_accept).
  • Posterior predictive checks: simulate replicated data from the posterior and compare with observed data (distribution, extremes, group patterns). Misfit means the model is wrong, not that sampling failed.
  • Model comparison: leave-one-out cross-validation (PSIS-LOO) or WAIC with az.compare; examine Pareto-k warnings; prefer predictive checks and domain reasoning over chasing small differences.
  • Check identifiability and parameter correlations; scale predictors.

Decisions

Because you have a full posterior you can compute probabilities of events (P(lift > 1 percent)), expected loss, and optimal actions under a utility function. Communicate uncertainty with intervals and probability statements, not just a point estimate.

Frequentist versus Bayesian

They often agree with ample data and weak priors. Bayesian methods shine with small data, hierarchical structures, prior knowledge, complex models and decision analysis; costs include computation, modelling choices that need justification, and communication.

Pitfalls

Ignoring convergence warnings, using default priors blindly, interpreting a posterior from a misspecified model as truth, and confusing credible intervals with confidence intervals.
