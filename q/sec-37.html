Compare Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) and Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC) in enterprise applications.

Security engineer · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · IAM · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

RBAC grants permissions through roles assigned to users, which is simple to administer and audit but suffers role explosion and coarse context. ABAC evaluates policies over attributes of the subject, resource, action and environment, enabling fine-grained dynamic rules at the cost of policy complexity. Many enterprises combine them, using roles as coarse attributes and ABAC for exceptions.

Explained in depth

RBAC (Role-Based Access Control)

Permissions are attached to roles; users get roles. Example: invoice_clerk can create and read invoices, finance_manager can also approve. NIST RBAC adds role hierarchies (manager inherits clerk), separation of duty constraints (the same person cannot create and approve) and sessions.

{ "roles": { "support_agent": ["ticket:read", "ticket:update"], "admin": ["*"] }, "assignments": { "alice": ["support_agent"] } }

Strengths: easy to understand, review and certify (access reviews list user to role), maps to job functions, mature tooling (IAM groups, Kubernetes RBAC, application frameworks), fast evaluation. Weaknesses:

  • Role explosion: needing "support agent for EU customers during business hours who can refund under 100" creates many roles (support_eu_refund_100).
  • Static: no context (time, location, device risk, data sensitivity, relationship to resource).
  • Resource-level rules ("only own records") need extra code.
  • Role creep: users accumulate roles over time, violating least privilege.

ABAC (Attribute-Based Access Control)

A policy is a rule over attributes:

  • Subject (department, clearance, role, employment type, MFA level),
  • Resource (owner, classification, region, status, tenant),
  • Action (read, approve, delete),
  • Environment (time, IP, device compliance, risk score).

Decision = policy evaluation (permit or deny).

permit(action == "read", resource.type == "patient_record")
  when subject.role == "doctor"
   and subject.department == resource.department
   and environment.time within 07:00-19:00
   and subject.mfa == true
forbid when resource.classification == "restricted" and subject.clearance < 3

Architecture (XACML terms): PEP (policy enforcement point, in the app or gateway) calls PDP (decision point) which uses PIP (attribute sources) and PAP (policy administration). Modern engines: Open Policy Agent (Rego), AWS Cedar and Verified Permissions, Casbin, Oso, Topaz, Google Zanzibar-style ReBAC systems (OpenFGA, SpiceDB) for relationship-based rules. Strengths: fine-grained, dynamic, fewer artefacts than roles, supports data-sensitive and contextual rules, centralised policy-as-code with testing and versioning, good for multi-tenant and regulated environments. Weaknesses: policies are harder to reason about and audit ("who can access X?" needs analysis tooling), attribute quality and freshness matter, performance and caching of attribute lookups, governance of policy sprawl, debugging overhead.

Hybrid approach

Use RBAC for coarse entitlements (job functions, application access) and ABAC or ReBAC for fine-grained, contextual, resource-level rules. Roles become attributes (subject.roles). Examples: AWS IAM combines policies with tag-based conditions (aws:PrincipalTag/team == aws:ResourceTag/team), Kubernetes RBAC plus admission policies for attributes, and Azure RBAC with ABAC conditions.

Design guidance

  • Centralise decisions (policy as code, tested with unit tests and policy simulation) but enforce at every layer (gateway, service, data); deny by default.
  • Keep roles few and business meaningful; apply separation of duties; automate joiner-mover-leaver and periodic access recertification.
  • For ABAC ensure reliable attribute sources (HR system, device posture), log decisions with the attributes used, and offer "why was this denied" explanations.
  • Use just-in-time elevation for privileged roles.

Pitfalls

Creating a new role for every exception, embedding authorisation checks in scattered code, trusting client-supplied attributes, stale attributes after job changes, and policy engines without test suites.
