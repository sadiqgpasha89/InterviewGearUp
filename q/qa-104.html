What is the difference between verification and validation, and between QA, QC and testing?

QA / SDET · Gear 1 (Junior) · Fundamentals · Difficulty: easy · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Verification asks whether we are building the product right, checking work against specifications through reviews, inspections and static analysis. Validation asks whether we are building the right product, checking by running it against user needs. QA is the process-focused effort to prevent defects, QC is the product-focused inspection that detects them, and testing is one QC activity.

Explained in depth

Verification versus validation

VerificationValidation
Question"Are we building the product right?""Are we building the right product?"
ChecksConformance to specification and standardsFit to user and business needs
MethodStatic: reviews, walkthroughs, inspections, static analysisDynamic: executing the software, demos, UAT
WhenThroughout development, earlyTypically after parts of the product are working
ExampleReviewing a requirements document for ambiguity; code reviewA user trying the checkout and confirming it solves their problem

A product can pass verification (matches the spec) and still fail validation (the spec was wrong).

QA, QC and testing

  • Quality assurance (QA): process-oriented and preventive. Defining standards, reviews, training, test strategy, and improving how software is built so defects are less likely. Everyone owns it.
  • Quality control (QC): product-oriented and detective. Inspecting and testing the product to find defects before release.
  • Testing: executing software (or analysing it) to find defects and gain information about quality. It is one activity within QC.

Example

  • QA: introduce code review rules, a definition of done and a CI pipeline.
  • QC: running regression and exploratory testing on the release candidate.
  • Verification: reviewing the design against requirements.
  • Validation: acceptance testing with customers.

In practice

Modern teams blur the lines (quality engineering embedded in development), but the vocabulary is common in interviews: preventing defects early is cheaper than finding them late.
