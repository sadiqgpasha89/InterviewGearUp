What is the difference between Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) and Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)?

Backend engineer · Gear 3 (Senior) · Security · Difficulty: easy · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

RBAC grants permissions to roles and assigns roles to users, which is simple and auditable. ABAC evaluates policies over attributes of the subject, resource, action and environment (department, ownership, sensitivity, time, location), allowing fine-grained, context-aware decisions at the cost of more complexity.

Explained in depth

RBAC

Users get roles (admin, editor, viewer); roles contain permissions (article:update). Access check: does any role of the user include the permission?

user alice -> role editor -> permissions {article:read, article:update}

Pros: easy to understand, review and audit, maps to organisational job functions, fast checks, supported everywhere (cloud IAM, Kubernetes RBAC, frameworks). Cons: coarse; cannot express "only edit your own articles", "only during business hours" or "only for customers in your region" without creating many roles (role explosion: editor-eu-draft, editor-us-published...).

ABAC

Decisions come from rules evaluated over attributes:

  • Subject: role, department, clearance, tenant.
  • Resource: owner, classification, status, region.
  • Action: read, approve, delete.
  • Environment: time, device trust, IP range, risk score.
permit if subject.department == resource.department
       and resource.status != "locked"
       and (action == "read" or subject.id == resource.ownerId)

Pros: very flexible, fewer roles, supports multi-tenancy, data classification and contextual rules, policies change without redeploying code. Cons: harder to reason about and audit ("who can access X?"), policy sprawl, attribute data must be accurate and available at decision time, and performance needs care.

Practical approach

Combine them: roles for broad capabilities plus attribute conditions for ownership and context (sometimes called RBAC with constraints, or relationship-based access control, ReBAC, as in Google Zanzibar, SpiceDB, OpenFGA for sharing models like "viewer of folder implies viewer of document").

Implementation guidance

  • Separate policy decision (a policy engine such as OPA with Rego, AWS Cedar, XACML, Casbin) from policy enforcement (middleware, gateway, data layer), and keep decisions centralised and testable.
  • Default deny, least privilege, explicit scopes in tokens, and re-check authorisation on the server for every request (never trust UI hiding).
  • Enforce row-level filters in queries (WHERE tenant_id = :t) or database row-level security, and handle object-level checks (IDOR prevention).
  • Log decisions with the reason, test policies with unit tests and simulation, and review role assignments regularly.
  • Cache decisions carefully with short TTLs and revoke on change.

Pitfalls

Role explosion, policies embedded in scattered if statements, stale attributes in JWTs (long-lived tokens carrying old roles), and checking only coarse permissions without verifying resource ownership.
