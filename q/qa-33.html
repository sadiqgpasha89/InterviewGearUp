What are the key performance testing metrics: Throughput (TPS/RPS), Latency percentiles (P95, P99), and Error rate?

QA / SDET · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Performance · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

The core performance metrics are throughput (requests or transactions per second), latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99, which show typical and tail response times) and error rate. Averages hide slow outliers, so judge latency by percentiles, and only trust throughput when the error rate is low, because failing fast can look like high throughput.

Explained in depth

Throughput

The amount of work completed per unit of time: requests per second (RPS), transactions per second (TPS) or bytes per second. Report successful throughput, since errors can complete quickly and inflate the raw number.

Latency and percentiles

Response time is a distribution, not a single value. A percentile says what fraction of requests were at or below a time:

  • p50 (median): the typical experience.
  • p95: 5 percent of requests are slower than this.
  • p99 and p99.9: the tail, which matters because users and services that make many calls hit slow ones often. If a page makes 100 calls, most page loads include at least one p99 call.

Averages hide the tail: 99 requests at 100 ms and one at 10 s give a mean of about 200 ms, which looks fine.

Error rate

The share of failed requests (HTTP 5xx, timeouts, assertion failures). Track it with throughput and latency, because under overload systems often return errors quickly, so latency looks good while users suffer.

Related measures

  • Concurrency (virtual users) and think time, which connect load to throughput by Little's law: concurrency = throughput * latency.
  • Saturation of CPU, memory, connection pools and queues on the system under test.
  • Apdex or SLOs, for example "99% of requests under 300 ms and under 0.1% errors".

Good practice

Measure from the client side, warm up first, keep the test long enough for steady state, avoid coordinated omission (open-model load generators such as k6 arrival-rate executors), and compare against a baseline.

Likely follow-ups

  • Why is the average response time misleading?
  • What is coordinated omission and how does it distort results?
  • How do you relate these metrics to service level objectives?
