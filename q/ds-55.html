How would you design experiments for a two-sided marketplace such as ride-hailing or delivery where user-level randomisation is biased by interference?

Data scientist · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Experimentation design · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

In marketplaces treatment on one side changes supply or demand for the other, so user-level A/B tests are biased. Use switchback tests that alternate treatment over time in a region, geo or cluster randomisation, budget-split or two-sided designs, and measure market-level metrics. Increase power with long windows and variance reduction, and use simulation and observational models to correct interference.

Explained in depth

The interference problem

In a two-sided marketplace, treatment and control users share finite resources: drivers, couriers, inventory, ad budgets, or demand. Examples:

  • A new pricing for riders in treatment draws more demand, using up drivers that control riders would have used, so control looks worse, inflating the measured lift (and the reverse for cannibalisation).
  • A ranking change that favours some sellers shifts demand away from others.
  • Matching algorithm changes affect everybody nearby.

The assumption of no interference (SUTVA) fails, and the A/B estimate does not equal the effect of launching to everyone (the global treatment effect).

Switchback specifics

  • Pick time blocks long enough that the effect of one period fades before the next (carryover), but short enough to get many randomisation units; common compromise is 30 to 120 minutes for dispatch, and day-level for slow systems.
  • Randomise or counterbalance the sequence (avoid always starting with treatment on Mondays) and stratify by hour-of-week; use a washout period at boundaries, dropping the first minutes after switch.
  • Analyse at the block level (market x time window) with regression: metric ~ treatment + market FE + hour-of-week FE, clustering standard errors by market-time units or using randomisation inference; account for autocorrelation (HAC).
  • Variance is high: use CUPED with pre-period of the same block, ratio estimators, and longer runs (weeks).

Metrics

Use market-level outcomes: trips completed per hour, wait time, utilisation, driver earnings, cancellations, GMV, plus guardrails on both sides (rider experience, driver supply). Look at short-run versus long-run effects: pricing changes alter behaviour over weeks (learning), and supply responds to earnings with delay.

Complementary evidence

Pair experiments with observational analyses, structural models of supply and demand elasticities, interference-aware estimators (exposure mapping), and sanity checks such as comparing user-level A/B to switchback results to estimate bias magnitude.

Pitfalls

Using user-level randomisation for market-wide algorithms, switching too frequently, ignoring day-of-week balance, and ignoring that effects at small scale may differ from a full launch (equilibrium effects).
