How do you embed security champions within product teams to foster a security-first engineering mindset?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Strategy · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Embed security champions by recruiting motivated volunteers from each product team, giving them training, time allowance, a direct line to the security team and clear responsibilities such as threat modeling, review support and triage. Recognise them in performance reviews, equip them with tools and playbooks, and measure outcomes like fixed findings and earlier detection.

Explained in depth

Why champions

A central security team cannot review every design and pull request in a growing organisation. Security champions are engineers (or product people) embedded in delivery teams who act as the local security voice: they raise awareness, apply secure practices, help triage findings, and connect the team with security specialists. This scales security expertise, shifts work left, and builds a security-first mindset (OWASP Security Champions Guide, BSIMM and OWASP SAMM describe the model).

Programme design

  1. Sponsor and goals: get executive and engineering leadership backing; define goals such as reduce time to fix vulnerabilities, increase threat model coverage, improve secure coding practices, and meet compliance requirements. Define success metrics.
  2. Select champions: volunteers with interest, credibility and communication skills, from every team (including mobile, data and infrastructure). Obtain manager buy-in and allocate time (typically 10 to 20 percent or a few hours per week), written into their goals. Avoid assigning unwilling people or making the role a dumping ground. Aim for roughly one champion per team of 8 to 10 engineers, and ensure diversity of experience.
  3. Role and responsibilities: clear charter:
  • Be the first point of contact for security questions and a liaison to the security team.
  • Help with threat modeling sessions for new features, reviewing designs, and ensuring security requirements are in tickets.
  • Promote secure coding and review pull requests for security issues (not as sole approver).
  • Triage and track findings from SAST, SCA, DAST, bug bounty and pentests, helping prioritise and ensuring fixes land.
  • Maintain the team's security backlog, participate in incident response and post-incident actions, and share lessons.
  • Evangelise: demos, lunch and learns, newsletters.

Emphasise enablement, not gatekeeping: champions help teams own security, they do not carry all responsibility.

  1. Training and growth: onboarding track (OWASP Top 10, secure SDLC, threat modeling basics, authentication and authorisation, secrets management, supply chain security, cloud security), hands-on labs (Secure Code Warrior, OWASP Juice Shop, PortSwigger Web Security Academy, HackTheBox), certifications or conference attendance, and security team office hours and mentoring. Offer advanced tracks (incident response, cloud security, cryptography).
  2. Community and communication: a private channel, regular (monthly) champions meetings with updates on threats and tooling, case studies from incidents, "capture the flag" events, and a shared knowledge base and playbooks (checklists, threat model templates, secure design patterns, code review guides). Provide direct escalation paths and fast answers from the security team.
  3. Tooling: integrate security tools in developer workflows (IDE plugins, pre-commit hooks, CI scans with clear and actionable findings), dashboards per team showing vulnerability trends and SLAs, and automation so champions can focus on judgement.
  4. Recognition and incentives: public acknowledgement, badges, certificates, inclusion in performance reviews and promotion criteria, learning budget, conference sponsorship, swag, direct access to leadership, and a visible career path into security engineering for those interested. Provide leadership support so security work is valued, not seen as extra to feature delivery.
  5. Avoid pitfalls: do not overload champions with all findings; keep expectations realistic; ensure the central security team continues to own policy, tooling, and high-risk reviews; rotate or refresh champions to avoid burnout; and keep the programme alive with fresh content.

Measure outcomes

  • Programme health: number of champions and coverage per team, attendance, training completion, satisfaction, retention in role.
  • Security outcomes: time to remediate vulnerabilities, backlog of open findings by age, percentage of features with threat models, vulnerability density in new code, incident count and severity, pen test findings trend, secret leaks caught pre-commit, dependency update lag, bug bounty valid reports.
  • Culture: survey on security confidence, number of security questions raised early, developers requesting reviews.

Review quarterly and adapt.

Pitfalls

Volunteers without time, no recognition, security team unresponsive, champions as policing agents, no clear scope, and treating the programme as a one-time training event.

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you select and motivate security champions without turning them into a bottleneck for all security tasks?
  • What training path and responsibilities should a champion have in the first year?
  • How do you measure whether the champions programme improves security outcomes?
