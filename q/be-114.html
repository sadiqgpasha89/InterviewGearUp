How do you design a backend to degrade gracefully and shed load when it is overloaded?

Backend engineer · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Reliability · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Protect the system by limiting concurrency and queue length, rejecting excess work early with 429 or 503, prioritising critical requests, using timeouts, circuit breakers and bulkheads, serving cached or reduced responses when dependencies fail, and applying adaptive controls such as backpressure and autoscaling. Test with load and chaos experiments so overload becomes a controlled degradation, not a collapse.

Explained in depth

The failure mode

When demand exceeds capacity, latency rises, queues grow, timeouts trigger retries, which add more load (a retry storm), and the service enters a metastable failure where it stays down even after the original spike ends. Goodput (useful work) collapses.

Principles

  1. Bound everything: limit concurrent requests, thread pools and queue sizes. An unbounded queue just converts overload into long latency, and requests are often abandoned by clients before being processed.
  2. Fail fast: reject excess work early and cheaply with 429 or 503 and Retry-After, rather than accepting and timing out.
  3. Timeouts and deadlines: every call has a timeout, and the remaining deadline is propagated downstream so work no one is waiting for is cancelled.
  4. Retries with discipline: exponential backoff with jitter, retry budgets (for example at most 10 percent extra traffic), and retries only at one layer.

Load shedding strategies

  • Priority classes: protect critical paths (checkout, login) and drop low-value traffic (recommendations, analytics, batch) first.
  • Adaptive concurrency limits (such as gradient-based limiters, CoDel-style queue delay) that find capacity automatically.
  • Per-client quotas and rate limits so one tenant cannot consume everything.
  • Shed based on cost: reject expensive requests when CPU is high.

Isolation and degradation

  • Bulkheads: separate thread or connection pools per dependency, so a slow one cannot exhaust all resources.
  • Circuit breakers: stop calling a failing dependency, return a fallback, and probe for recovery.
  • Graceful degradation: serve cached or stale data, disable optional features (feature flags), simplify responses, or queue non-urgent work.
  • Backpressure: propagate slowness upstream through bounded queues and streaming flow control.

Capacity and operations

Autoscaling (scale on queue depth or latency, with warm-up time), headroom for spikes, and load tests beyond expected peak. Alert on saturation signals (queue time, CPU, pool usage), keep runbooks, and run game days to verify the breakers and shedding rules behave.
