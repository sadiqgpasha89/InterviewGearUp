Explain Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs): Minimax game between Generator and Discriminator.

ML / AI engineer · Gear 3 (Senior) · Generative · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

A GAN pits a generator that maps noise to fake samples against a discriminator that classifies real versus fake. They play a minimax game: the discriminator maximises its classification accuracy and the generator minimises it, so each improves. At equilibrium the generator matches the data distribution. Training is unstable, with mode collapse and vanishing gradients, mitigated by variants such as WGAN.

Explained in depth

Setup

  • Generator G(z; θ_g): takes noise z ~ p_z (Gaussian) and outputs a synthetic sample G(z) (an image).
  • Discriminator D(x; θ_d): outputs the probability that x came from the real data distribution p_data rather than from G.

Minimax objective (Goodfellow et al., 2014)

min_G max_D V(D, G) = E_{x ~ p_data}[ log D(x) ] + E_{z ~ p_z}[ log(1 − D(G(z))) ]

  • D wants to maximise V: output near 1 on real data, near 0 on fakes.
  • G wants to minimise V: make D(G(z)) close to 1 (fool D).

For a fixed G, the optimal discriminator is D*(x) = p_data(x) / (p_data(x) + p_g(x)). Plugging in, G minimises the Jensen-Shannon divergence between p_data and p_g; the global optimum is p_g = p_data with D* = 0.5 everywhere (D cannot tell them apart).

Training loop

Alternate:

  1. Update D for k steps: sample real and fake batches, ascend log D(x) + log(1 − D(G(z))).
  2. Update G: sample noise, descend log(1 − D(G(z))). In practice use the non-saturating loss max log D(G(z)) because early on D easily rejects fakes, making log(1 − D(G(z))) saturate with vanishing gradients.

Why GANs are hard to train

  • Non-convergence and oscillation: simultaneous gradient descent on a min-max game need not converge (cycling).
  • Vanishing gradients: if D becomes too strong, G receives little gradient; the JS divergence is constant when distributions have disjoint supports.
  • Mode collapse: G produces a limited variety of outputs (a few faces), since fooling D with a single good mode is enough.
  • Sensitivity to architecture, learning rates, batch size, normalisation; no loss curve that reliably indicates sample quality.
  • Evaluation difficulty: use FID (Fréchet Inception Distance), Inception Score, precision and recall for distributions, human evaluation.

Stabilisation techniques and variants

  • DCGAN guidelines (strided convolutions, batch norm, LeakyReLU), label smoothing, instance noise, two time-scale update rule (TTUR), spectral normalisation, minibatch discrimination, feature matching.
  • WGAN / WGAN-GP: replace JS with the Wasserstein-1 (earth mover) distance; the discriminator becomes a critic with Lipschitz constraint (weight clipping or gradient penalty), providing meaningful gradients even for disjoint supports and a loss that correlates with quality.
  • LSGAN (least squares loss), Hinge loss (BigGAN), R1 regularisation.
  • Conditional GANs (cGAN, Pix2Pix, CycleGAN for unpaired image translation), Progressive GAN, StyleGAN 1/2/3 (high-resolution faces with style-based control), BigGAN, GigaGAN.

Position today

GANs produce sharp images quickly (single forward pass), good for real-time upscaling, super-resolution, domain translation, data augmentation, and synthetic data. Diffusion models now dominate image generation because they train stably, cover modes better and scale with text conditioning, but they are slower to sample; VAEs give explicit latent spaces and likelihood bounds but blurrier samples; autoregressive and flow models offer exact likelihoods. Adversarial losses are still used as components (VQGAN decoders, latent diffusion autoencoders, perceptual and adversarial losses in video and speech codecs).

Pitfalls

Over-training the discriminator, evaluating only by eye, ignoring mode coverage, leaking training samples (memorisation) and ethical misuse (deepfakes).
