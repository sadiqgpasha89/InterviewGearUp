When do deep neural networks outperform gradient boosted trees on tabular data, and how do you decide which to use?

Data scientist · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Machine learning algorithms · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Gradient boosted trees remain the default for medium-sized tabular data thanks to accuracy, speed and robustness to messy features. Neural networks win with very large datasets, high-cardinality categorical embeddings, multimodal inputs like text and images, sequences, multi-task learning or the need for end-to-end differentiable components. Benchmark both with the same validation and consider ensembles.

Explained in depth

Empirical picture

Benchmarks (for example Grinsztajn et al. 2022 and later studies) repeatedly find that tree ensembles (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, random forests) match or beat deep learning on typical tabular datasets with up to tens or hundreds of thousands of rows, with less tuning. Newer tabular deep models (TabNet, FT-Transformer, TabPFN, ResNet-style MLPs, NODE) narrow the gap or win in some settings, especially with large data or pretraining.

Why trees do well on tabular data

  • Tabular features are heterogeneous (counts, categories, ratios) with different scales and meanings; trees handle them without normalisation.
  • Many features are irrelevant or uninformative; trees ignore them naturally, while MLPs can be hurt.
  • Target functions are often irregular, with sharp thresholds (rule-like), where trees are better than smooth neural functions.
  • Robust to outliers, missing values and monotone transformations; fewer hyperparameters; train quickly on CPUs; strong out-of-the-box performance; easy feature importance and SHAP.

When neural networks make sense

SituationReason
Very large data (millions to billions of rows)networks keep improving with scale and can be trained online
High-cardinality categorical features (user IDs, item IDs)learned embeddings share information and generalise; recommender and CTR models (DeepFM, DLRM, two-tower)
Multimodal inputs (text, images, audio plus tabular)end-to-end training with pretrained encoders
Sequences and time-series with rich dynamicsRNNs, transformers, temporal convolutions
Multi-task learning / shared representationsone network predicts several outcomes
Need for differentiable pipelinesembedding retrieval, learning-to-rank losses, reinforcement learning, custom losses with constraints
Transfer or pretraining (self-supervised on unlabelled tabular rows, foundation models like TabPFN for small data)leverages prior knowledge
Streaming / continual learningonline gradient updates

Small data (a few hundred rows) often favours regularised linear models or Bayesian methods; TabPFN-style models can also be strong.

How to decide (practical process)

  1. Establish simple baselines: logistic or linear regression, then LightGBM or CatBoost with sensible defaults and early stopping.
  2. Invest in features and validation first; model class changes yield smaller gains than good data.
  3. If there is a reason to expect benefits from neural nets (above), try an MLP with embeddings, batch norm or layer norm, dropout, weight decay, a learning-rate schedule, quantile or rank transformation of numerics, and early stopping; tune with the same CV.
  4. Compare with identical splits and metrics, include latency, memory, training cost, maintenance, and monitoring needs.
  5. Consider ensembling or stacking (GBDT plus NN predictions often gives a small boost because errors differ) when the gain justifies complexity; or use a neural embedding as features for the GBDT.
import torch.nn as nn
class TabNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, cat_cards, n_num, emb=16, hidden=256):
        super().__init__()
        self.embs = nn.ModuleList([nn.Embedding(c, min(emb, (c + 1) // 2)) for c in cat_cards])
        d = sum(e.embedding_dim for e in self.embs) + n_num
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, hidden), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.2), nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(hidden, 1))
    def forward(self, xcat, xnum):
        z = torch.cat([e(xcat[:, i]) for i, e in enumerate(self.embs)] + [xnum], dim=1)
        return self.mlp(z).squeeze(-1)

Operational factors

Trees: simple deployment, fast inference, easier debugging, native handling of missing values, monotonic constraints. Networks: GPU or optimised serving, sensitivity to preprocessing, more hyperparameters, higher variance across seeds (average seeds), need for careful calibration.

Pitfalls

Assuming deep learning is automatically better, comparing tuned networks with untuned trees (or the reverse), skipping feature engineering, and ignoring serving cost and interpretability.
