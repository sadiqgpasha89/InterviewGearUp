How do you run asynchronous standups and reduce calendar fatigue from excessive recurring meetings?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Culture · Difficulty: easy · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Replace many live standups with an asynchronous written update, using a consistent template for progress, plans and blockers in a tool or channel, keep a short live sync only when needed, and cut meeting load by auditing recurring meetings, setting agendas and owners, defaulting to 25 or 50 minute slots, adding no-meeting focus blocks, and recording decisions in documents.

Explained in depth

Why change

Daily live standups often become status reports to the manager, interrupt deep work, are hard across time zones, and are mostly useful only for a few blockers. Calendar fatigue from recurring meetings fragments focus time, which is critical for engineering. Async updates save time, create a searchable record, and include remote and part-time colleagues equally.

Asynchronous standup design

  1. Choose the medium: a dedicated Slack or Teams channel using a workflow or bot (Geekbot, Standuply, Slack Workflow Builder, Range, Status Hero, Jira or Linear daily summaries), a threaded post in a team channel, or a shared doc. Pick one place.
  2. Template (takes 2 minutes):
  • Done since last update (link PRs or tickets).
  • Doing next (top 1 to 3 items).
  • Blockers or help needed (tag the person who can help).
  • Optional: risks, FYI (out of office), a mood or energy check.

Focus on outcomes and blockers, not hour-by-hour lists. Use automation to pull commits, PRs and ticket changes, reducing manual writing.

  1. Cadence and deadlines: daily or three times per week, posted at the person's start of day, with a reasonable window (for example by 11 am local). Not everyone must read every update; teammates scan for blockers.
  2. Follow-up on blockers: a designated person (rotating "triage owner" or the lead) replies to blockers within a few hours, creates threads, and escalates. Make it clear that blockers receive timely responses, otherwise people stop reporting.
  3. Add a short live sync only when valuable: for a team in the same time zone, a weekly 15 to 30 minute "huddle" to discuss priorities, demos, risks and relationships; or an optional ad-hoc call when blockers need discussion. Rotate facilitators.
  4. Avoid status theatre: leaders do not use updates for micromanagement or counting tasks; they use them to remove blockers. Keep updates short, celebrate help provided, and review the format regularly via retrospectives.

Reducing meeting overload

  1. Audit: export the calendar for the team for 4 weeks; list recurring meetings with purpose, attendees, cost (attendees x hours), outcomes, and ratings. Ask the team to flag meetings that are not useful. Use tools (Clockwise, Reclaim, Google Calendar insights).
  2. Apply rules:
  • Every meeting needs a clear purpose, owner, agenda, and decision or outcome. No agenda, no meeting.
  • Prefer async alternatives first: docs, RFC comments, recorded demos (Loom), written updates, polls. Use meetings for discussion, conflict resolution, brainstorming and relationship building.
  • Shorten defaults to 25 or 50 minutes, give breaks between meetings, and limit attendees to those who must contribute (optional attendees can read notes).
  • Cancel or reduce recurring meetings that lack outcomes; set review dates and end dates for series.
  • No-meeting blocks: protect focus time (for example Wednesdays, or mornings) team-wide.
  • Meeting-free days or "maker's schedule" for engineers; bundle meetings on specific days.
  • Share notes and recordings, with action items, so absentees can catch up.
  • Respect time zones and avoid after-hours meetings.
  1. Set norms: team charter on meeting etiquette, response times, camera, and use of docs. Managers model good practice by cancelling their own unnecessary meetings.
  2. Keep important rituals: sprint planning, retrospectives, demos, one-to-ones, incident reviews, and design reviews remain valuable but should be efficient. Use pre-reads and time-boxed decisions.

Measure

Hours in meetings per engineer per week, focus time blocks, survey ratings of meeting usefulness, blockers resolution time, and delivery metrics. Aim for noticeable improvements (for example at least 4 uninterrupted hours per day on four days).

When synchronous is better

Complex, ambiguous or emotional topics, incident response, conflict resolution, early-stage brainstorming, onboarding and team building, sensitive feedback, and urgent decisions.

Pitfalls

Async updates that nobody reads, no response to blockers, converting every meeting into a long document, abandoning team connection entirely, and exempting managers from meeting discipline.
