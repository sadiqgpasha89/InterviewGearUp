Explain Public Key Infrastructure (PKI): Certificate Authorities (CA), Root Certificates, and CRLs/OCSP.

Security engineer · Gear 3 (Senior) · Cryptography · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

PKI binds public keys to identities through certificates signed by Certificate Authorities. A client trusts root CA certificates preinstalled in its trust store; a server presents a chain from its certificate through intermediate CAs to a trusted root, and the client verifies signatures, validity dates, name and revocation status. Revocation is checked using CRLs or OCSP.

Explained in depth

Components

  • Certificate: an X.509 document containing a subject name (or DNS names in Subject Alternative Names), the subject's public key, validity period, key usages and the issuer's signature.
  • Certificate Authority (CA): the trusted party that verifies identity and signs certificates.
  • Root CA: a self-signed certificate held in the operating system or browser trust store. Roots are kept offline in HSMs and rarely used directly.
  • Intermediate CAs: signed by the root and used for day-to-day issuance, so a compromise can be contained by revoking an intermediate without replacing a root.
  • Registration authority and policies (Certificate Policy and Practice Statement) define validation levels (domain, organisation, extended).

Chain validation

The server sends its leaf certificate and intermediates. The client checks:

  1. Each certificate is signed by the next one up the chain, ending at a trusted root.
  2. Dates are valid, and key usage and basic constraints allow the use.
  3. The hostname matches the SAN.
  4. The certificate is not revoked.

Revocation

  • CRL (Certificate Revocation List): a signed list of revoked serial numbers published by the CA; can be large and stale.
  • OCSP: ask the CA's responder about one certificate; real time but adds latency and leaks browsing to the CA. OCSP stapling lets the server fetch and attach a signed, time-stamped response, fixing both issues.
  • Browsers also use curated lists (such as CRLite) and short-lived certificates (90 days or less, automated with ACME) reduce the need for revocation.

Other controls

Certificate Transparency logs make misissued certificates publicly detectable; CAA DNS records restrict which CAs may issue for a domain; private PKI (internal CAs) secures mTLS inside organisations.
