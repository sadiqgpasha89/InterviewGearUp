How do you establish equitable on-call compensation, secondary escalation paths, and paging policies?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Operations · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Design on-call to be sustainable and fair: schedule rotations with enough people for a safe frequency, define primary and secondary roles with clear escalation paths, compensate through pay, time off or both, set paging policies that page only on actionable user-impacting alerts, track toil and alert quality, and invest in reliability work to reduce pages.

Explained in depth

Principles

On-call is real work that constrains personal time and carries stress; it must be voluntary where possible, fairly distributed, compensated, supported by good tooling and runbooks, and continuously improved. Google SRE guidance suggests limiting on-call to about 25 percent of an engineer's time, with no more than two incidents per shift on average so each can be handled properly and followed up.

Rotation design

  1. Team size and frequency: for 24x7 coverage with primary and secondary, aim for at least 8 engineers in a rotation (one week of primary every 8 weeks), or consider follow-the-sun across regions (each region covers local daytime) to avoid night pages. Small teams can pool into a shared rotation across related services.
  2. Roles:
  • Primary: first responder, acknowledges within the target (for example 5 minutes), triages and mitigates.
  • Secondary / backup: automatically paged if primary does not acknowledge, and available for escalation or pairing.
  • Escalation path: subject matter experts, engineering manager, incident commander for major incidents, executive for customer-impacting severity 1.
  • Ensure shadowing and training for new on-call engineers (shadow, then reverse-shadow), plus access, tooling and documentation checks before they are scheduled.
  1. Scheduling fairness: publish schedules weeks ahead, allow swaps easily, respect holidays and personal constraints, account for time zones, and avoid back-to-back weeks. Handoff meetings or notes at rotation change to review ongoing issues. Exempt people temporarily for health, parental leave or after a heavy shift.
  2. Rest after incidents: if paged overnight, allow a late start or day off; require rest periods (for example 11 hours between shifts) and limit consecutive nights.

Compensation

Options (often combined):

  • On-call stipend: fixed pay per week or per day (for example a weekend premium and a smaller weekday amount), recognising availability constraints.
  • Incident pay: hourly or per-page pay for time worked outside hours, possibly at overtime rates (check labour law: in some countries on-call time and callouts have legal requirements, such as the EU Working Time Directive, UK and Germany rules).
  • Time off in lieu: compensatory days after heavy shifts or weekends.
  • Career and recognition: include on-call contribution in performance and promotion criteria, and don't penalise those who go on leave to recover.

Choose based on culture and law, keep rules transparent and consistent, and apply to everyone, including senior engineers and managers who join rotations to model fairness. Budget for it, because underfunded on-call becomes unpaid overtime.

Paging policy and alert quality

  • Page only for urgent, actionable, user-impacting problems (SLO burn, outage, data loss, security events). Everything else becomes a ticket or a business-hours notification. Use severity levels with matching notification channels.
  • Alert on symptoms (SLOs, error budget burn rates) instead of causes (CPU), include a runbook link, dashboard, and context in each alert, deduplicate and group related alerts, and use maintenance windows.
  • Define acknowledge and escalate timers (for example page, then re-page after 5 minutes, then secondary after 10, then manager).
  • Review alerts weekly: delete or tune noisy ones, measure pages per shift, off-hours pages, actionable percentage, mean time to acknowledge and resolve, and repeat offenders. Set targets such as "fewer than 2 pages per shift" and treat violations as reliability work.
  • Provide good tooling: PagerDuty, Opsgenie or incident.io, ChatOps, runbooks and automation (auto-remediation for common issues), access to logs and traces, and a clear incident process (incident commander, comms lead).
  • Run blameless postmortems and track action items; allocate sprint capacity to fix root causes.

Culture

Encourage asking for help, no hero culture, and escalate early. Provide support after tough incidents and mental health resources. Rotate ownership of reliability improvements so the burden is shared. Managers should take their turn on-call or the escalation role.

Pitfalls

Tiny rotations, paging on every anomaly, no secondary, unpaid weekends, no rest after incidents, and ignoring alert fatigue until engineers leave.

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you design alerting and paging policies so that people are paged only when action is needed?
  • How do you prevent on-call from burning out engineers and discouraging people from owning production?
