Explain the difference between correlation and causation, and what confounders and Simpson's paradox are.

Data scientist · Gear 1 (Junior) · Statistics · Difficulty: easy · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Correlation means two variables move together; causation means changing one produces a change in the other. A confounder influences both variables and creates a misleading association, and Simpson's paradox shows a trend reversing once data are split into groups. Randomised experiments or careful causal methods are needed to claim causation.

Explained in depth

Correlation versus causation

  • Correlation (Pearson r for linear relationships, Spearman for monotonic) measures association strength and direction. It is symmetric and says nothing about mechanism.
  • Causation means an intervention on X changes Y. The question is counterfactual: what would Y have been had X been different?

Why an association may exist without X causing Y:

  1. Confounding: a third variable Z causes both. Ice cream sales and drownings correlate because hot weather drives both.
  2. Reverse causation: Y causes X (people who feel unwell take more medicine; the medicine did not cause the illness).
  3. Selection bias (including collider bias): conditioning on a variable influenced by both X and Y creates a spurious link.
  4. Coincidence: with many variables, some spurious correlations appear by chance (Tyler Vigen's charts).

Confounders in business data

  • Customers who use a premium feature churn less, but engaged users are the ones who find and use premium features; engagement confounds.
  • Hospitals with more doctors have higher mortality because they receive sicker patients (severity confounds).
  • Marketing emails appear to boost sales, but they are sent to already-likely buyers.

Simpson's paradox

A trend present in aggregated data reverses in subgroups. Classic example: UC Berkeley admissions appeared biased against women overall, but within most departments women were admitted at equal or higher rates; women applied more to competitive departments. Numeric toy example: Treatment A cures 78 percent overall versus B 83 percent, yet A is better for both small and large kidney stones, because A was given more often to the harder large stones. The group-level analysis (conditioning on severity) is the right one if severity is a confounder. Whether to aggregate or stratify depends on the causal structure, not statistics alone, so draw a causal diagram (DAG).

How to get at causation

ApproachIdea
Randomised controlled trial / A/B testrandom assignment balances all confounders (known and unknown) in expectation
Adjustmentregression or stratification controlling for measured confounders (needs no unmeasured confounding)
Matching / propensity scores / inverse probability weightingcompare similar treated and untreated units
Difference-in-differencescompare before-after changes in treated versus control groups (parallel trends assumption)
Regression discontinuityexploit a cutoff rule (scholarship at score 80)
Instrumental variablesa variable that affects treatment but not the outcome directly
Synthetic controlbuild a weighted control from other units
Domain knowledge and DAGsdecide what to adjust for (confounders) and what not (colliders, mediators)

How to talk about it

Say "associated with" unless there is experimental or strong quasi-experimental evidence, state the assumptions, and propose the experiment that would settle it.

Pitfalls

Adjusting for colliders or mediators, believing regression alone proves causation, and ignoring unmeasured confounding.

Likely follow-ups

  • How does randomisation remove confounding in an A/B test?
  • Give an example where a lurking variable explains an apparent effect.
  • What do you do when you cannot run an experiment but must estimate an effect?
