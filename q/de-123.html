How do you decide whether to build or buy components of a data platform, and manage vendor lock-in?

Data engineer · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Strategy · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Buy for commodity capabilities that do not differentiate your business, such as ingestion connectors, warehouses and orchestration hosting, and build where your needs are unique or scale and cost make vendors prohibitive. Evaluate total cost of ownership, team skills, integration, security and exit cost, prefer open formats and standards to keep options open, and revisit the decision as scale and vendors change.

Explained in depth

Decision framework

  1. Is it a differentiator? Custom logic that gives competitive advantage justifies building. Commodity capabilities (connectors to SaaS tools, scheduling, monitoring) rarely do.
  2. Total cost of ownership (TCO) over 3 to 5 years:
  • Buy: licences or usage, implementation, integration, training, premium support, price increases, egress fees.
  • Build: engineers (design, build, run, on-call, upgrades, security patches), infrastructure, opportunity cost, and key-person risk.
  1. Time to value: buying is usually faster; building needs ongoing capacity.
  2. Fit and flexibility: functional gaps, customisation needs, performance at your scale, supported sources and destinations, roadmap alignment, and APIs.
  3. Security and compliance: certifications (SOC 2, ISO 27001), data residency, encryption and private networking, audit logging, and vendor access to your data.
  4. Skills and team size: a small team should buy more; a large mature team can run open source.
  5. Scale economics: vendor usage-based pricing can become very expensive at large volumes, where self-managed open source becomes attractive.
  6. Vendor viability and support quality.

A middle path: managed open source

Run open standards (Spark, Kafka, Trino, Iceberg, dbt, Airflow) on managed services or vendor distributions to get operational relief while preserving portability.

Managing lock-in

  • Store data in open formats and table standards (Parquet, Iceberg, Delta) on storage you control.
  • Keep transformation logic in portable SQL and dbt, with abstraction between tools via standard interfaces (OpenLineage, OpenTelemetry, ANSI SQL, JDBC).
  • Understand exit costs: data export, egress charges, rewriting proprietary code and retraining. Negotiate contract terms (term lengths, price caps, data export rights).
  • Avoid deep use of proprietary features unless the benefit is clear, and document those used.

Process

Run a proof of concept with realistic data, workloads and success criteria; score options against weighted criteria; get security and finance review; record the decision in an ADR; and schedule a review at 12 months as needs evolve.
