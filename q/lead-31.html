What criteria determine a Go/No-Go release decision, and how do you design emergency rollback plans?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Delivery · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

A Go/No-Go decision weighs readiness criteria: passing tests and quality gates, no open critical defects, performance and security sign-off, operational readiness with monitoring, runbooks and on-call coverage, a tested rollback plan, and acceptable business risk. Plan rollbacks with feature flags, backward-compatible migrations and defined triggers so a bad release can be reversed quickly.

Explained in depth

Go/No-Go criteria

A release readiness review (often 24 to 48 hours before launch, with a final check just before) evaluates a checklist with clear, measurable thresholds. Typical categories:

  1. Functionality and quality: all must-have scope complete, no open P0 or P1 defects, known issues documented with workarounds, regression, integration and end-to-end suites passing, UAT sign-off, test coverage and quality gate status, exploratory testing for risky areas.
  2. Non-functional: performance and load tests meet SLOs, capacity validated for expected traffic (plus headroom), resilience and failover tests, accessibility and compatibility.
  3. Security and compliance: threat model, SAST/DAST and dependency scans clean for critical issues, penetration test findings addressed, privacy review, legal or regulatory approvals, audit evidence.
  4. Operational readiness: monitoring, dashboards and alerts for the new components, logs and traces, runbooks, on-call staffed with trained people, escalation paths, support and customer success trained with FAQs, status page and communication templates ready, capacity and cost reviewed, dependencies and third parties confirmed.
  5. Deployment readiness: deployment automation rehearsed in staging, migrations tested on production-like data, configuration and secrets verified, feature flags set, dependency and version compatibility, deploy window chosen (avoid Fridays and peak periods unless justified).
  6. Business readiness: stakeholders aligned on launch plan, marketing and sales messages, documentation, rollout plan (percentage, regions), success metrics defined.
  7. Risk assessment: list known risks, likelihood, impact and mitigations; confirm the residual risk is acceptable to the business owner.

Decision making

Define the decider in advance (release manager or engineering lead with the product owner and operations/SRE input; for high-risk launches a change advisory or launch review board). Use a consensus-with-veto model for safety areas: security, SRE or QA can raise a No-Go for unmet critical criteria. Record the decision, rationale, conditions (for example "Go with flag off for region X"), and owners for follow-ups. Prevent schedule pressure from overriding: criteria agreed early, evidence visible to everyone, and an explicit risk acceptance by a named executive if a criterion is waived.

Designing the rollback plan

  1. Make rollback possible by design:
  • Backward-compatible database changes using expand and contract: add columns or tables first, deploy code that works with both schemas, migrate data, then remove old structures in a later release. Avoid destructive migrations in the same release as code.
  • Feature flags to disable new behaviour instantly without redeploying (kill switch), with defaults tested.
  • Versioned APIs and messages and tolerant readers so old and new versions interoperate.
  • Immutable artifacts and previous versions retained so redeploying the last known good build takes minutes; keep container images and configuration versioned.
  1. Progressive delivery: canary (1 to 5 percent), blue/green, or ring-based rollout with automated analysis (Argo Rollouts, Flagger, Spinnaker) comparing error rates and latency with baseline and auto-rollback when thresholds break.
  2. Define triggers: for example error rate > 2x baseline for 5 minutes, p99 latency above SLO, failed health checks, payment failure rate increase, data inconsistency detection, critical bug reports, or loss of key business KPIs. Pre-assign who can call a rollback (any on-call engineer, without waiting for approval).
  3. Document and rehearse: step-by-step rollback runbook, tested in staging, with estimated time and dependencies (data migrations needing reverse scripts, cache invalidation, message queue draining), and a decision tree for roll back versus roll forward (fix forward may be faster for small defects when the pipeline is quick; rollback when impact is large or the cause is unclear).
  4. Data considerations: if irreversible data changes happen, take backups or snapshots beforehand, plan for dual writes, and define how to repair data created by the faulty version.
  5. Communication plan: incident channel, stakeholders, customer comms and status page updates, and a post-release review.

During and after launch

A launch room with a clear owner, monitoring dashboards, a timeline checklist, and hypercare period (extra support for 24 to 72 hours). Run a post-launch retrospective and update criteria based on lessons.

Pitfalls

Subjective "feels ready" decisions, ignoring operational readiness, no tested rollback, irreversible migrations bundled with code, pressure to launch on the date regardless, and unclear authority to abort.
