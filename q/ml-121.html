How do you make a credit or collections risk model fair, explainable and compliant?

ML / AI engineer · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Ethics · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Exclude protected attributes and test for proxies; measure outcome and error-rate gaps across groups or segments (region, company size) where lawful; explain each decision with reason codes (for example from SHAP) that a person can act on; keep humans in the loop for adverse actions; and document data, validation and monitoring for model risk review. Legal requirements differ by country and customer type.

Explained in depth

Know the rules

  • Consumer credit is heavily regulated (for example adverse-action notices in the United States, and automated-decision rights under the GDPR in the EU). Business-to-business collections face fewer specific rules but still contract, debt-collection and anti-discrimination law. Involve legal and compliance early; this answer is not legal advice.

Fairness

  • Do not use protected attributes as features, and look for proxies (location, language, some industries) that reproduce them.
  • Measure, by group where lawful and by segment (region, company size, tenure): approval or action rates, false positive and false negative rates, and calibration. Different fairness definitions conflict, so choose the one that fits the harm (for collections, unequal false positive rates mean some groups are chased unfairly).
  • Mitigate by removing or transforming features, reweighting, constraints during training or group-aware thresholds where permitted, and re-check accuracy after each change.

Explainability

  • Global: feature importance and partial dependence for reviewers.
  • Local: SHAP values per decision translated into reason codes ('payment delays increased over the last three invoices'), not raw feature names.
  • Prefer features people can understand and act on; monotonic constraints (risk never falls as days late rise) make behaviour predictable.

Human oversight

  • Soft actions (reminders) can be automatic; adverse actions (suspension, sending to an agency, cutting credit) get human review and a way for the customer to dispute.

Governance

  • Model documentation: purpose, data, exclusions, validation results by segment, limitations.
  • Independent validation before launch, monitoring of fairness metrics in production, and an audit log of each decision with model version and reason codes.

Likely follow-ups

  • Why can equal calibration and equal false positive rates not always hold together?
  • How would you find proxy variables?
  • What makes a reason code useful to a customer?
