How do you cultivate an RFC (Request for Comments) culture for collaborative technical proposals?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Strategy · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

An RFC culture makes technical decisions visible and collaborative by having authors write a proposal with context, goals, alternatives and trade-offs, circulating it for time-boxed asynchronous review, recording decisions and rationale in a searchable repository, and defining who decides. Keep it lightweight, scale it by risk, and reward feedback so it informs rather than blocks.

Explained in depth

Why RFCs

Without a written process, decisions happen in hallway conversations and Slack threads, affected teams learn too late, rationale is lost, and loud voices win. An RFC (Request for Comments) or design document gives transparency, early feedback, shared context, institutional memory (a decision log), and a way for quieter and remote engineers to contribute. Examples: Rust RFCs, Python PEPs, Uber and Google design docs, ADRs (Architecture Decision Records).

Core components

  1. Template (short, one to five pages): title, author, status, reviewers, and date; context and problem; goals and non-goals; proposal (design, diagrams, API changes); alternatives considered with trade-offs; impact (security, cost, performance, operations, migration, rollout, testing, compatibility); risks and open questions; decision and rationale section filled at the end.
  2. Lifecycle with explicit states: Draft, In Review, Accepted, Rejected, Implemented, Superseded. Keep RFCs in a Git repository or wiki with numbered documents and search.
  3. Process:
  • Author discusses the idea informally first, then writes the RFC.
  • Circulate to affected teams, security, SRE and data owners with a review period (for example 5 business days; shorter for small changes) and a named decision maker or approvers (tech lead, architecture group, or consensus-with-decision-owner using the "disagree and commit" principle).
  • Comments happen asynchronously on the document or PR; a synchronous review meeting is held only for unresolved contentious points.
  • Record the outcome, update status, and link implementation tickets and ADRs.
  1. Thresholds: require RFCs for changes that are hard to reverse or cross team boundaries: new services or datastores, public API changes, major dependencies, security-sensitive designs, migrations, org-wide standards. Skip them for local, easily reversible changes (a short ADR or PR description is enough). Scale by risk: "one-way doors" get thorough review, "two-way doors" decide quickly (Bezos).

Making it healthy

  • Be lightweight: time-box reviews, provide templates and examples, and accept that a proposal need not be perfect to share ("strawman first").
  • Clear decision rights prevent infinite debate: define the approver in advance, set a deadline, and make a decision even when consensus is incomplete, documenting dissent.
  • Review etiquette: critique the design, not the person; ask questions first; label comments (blocking vs suggestion vs nit); separate "must fix" from preferences; senior reviewers model constructive feedback.
  • Reward participation: recognise high-quality RFC authors and reviewers in performance reviews and promotion criteria (especially for staff and principal levels), call out good examples in all-hands.
  • Keep it discoverable: central index, tagging, notifications to relevant channels, an "RFC digest" newsletter, and office hours with architects for coaching.
  • Close the loop: review outcomes after implementation (did the design deliver?), retire stale RFCs, and use lessons in future templates.

Rollout plan

Pilot with a few teams on real decisions, collect feedback after a quarter, adjust the template and thresholds, then publish guidelines. Leaders should write RFCs themselves and refuse to make major architectural decisions outside the process. For seniors who prefer verbal decisions, show the value: fewer re-litigated debates, faster onboarding, and protection when decisions are questioned later.

Metrics

Number of RFCs, time from draft to decision, participation breadth, proportion of production incidents linked to unreviewed designs, survey scores on decision transparency.

Pitfalls

Requiring RFCs for everything, vague ownership, review that takes weeks, RFCs written after implementation (rubber stamps), no archive, and hierarchy overriding written conclusions.
