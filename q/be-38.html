How does service discovery work in microservices using client-side vs. server-side approaches?

Backend engineer · Gear 3 (Senior) · Architecture · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Service discovery lets services find each other's changing network locations. With client-side discovery the caller queries a registry (Consul, Eureka, etcd) and load-balances itself. With server-side discovery the caller hits a stable address (load balancer, Kubernetes Service, service mesh) that consults the registry and routes the request.

Explained in depth

Why it is needed

In containerised and autoscaled environments, instances start, stop and move constantly, so IP addresses are ephemeral. Hardcoded hostnames and static config do not work.

Registry

Instances register on startup (self-registration) or a platform component registers them (third-party registration), send heartbeats or are health-checked, and are deregistered on shutdown or failure. Examples: Consul, etcd, ZooKeeper, Netflix Eureka, AWS Cloud Map, Kubernetes API (Endpoints and EndpointSlices).

Client-side discovery

client -> registry: "where is payment-service?" -> [10.0.1.5, 10.0.2.9, 10.0.3.2]
client picks one (round robin, least connections, zone aware) and calls it directly

Pros: no extra network hop, fine-grained, client-aware balancing, can do retries and hedging. Cons: discovery and balancing logic must exist in every language and client library (Ribbon, Spring Cloud LoadBalancer, gRPC resolvers), clients must cache and handle stale data, and the registry becomes a dependency.

Server-side discovery

client -> load balancer / virtual IP (stable) -> LB queries registry -> instance

Examples: AWS ELB with target groups, NGINX or HAProxy with Consul-template, and Kubernetes Services: a Service has a stable ClusterIP and DNS name (payment.default.svc.cluster.local); kube-proxy (iptables, IPVS or eBPF in Cilium) routes to healthy pod IPs listed in EndpointSlices, using readiness probes to remove unready pods. Pros: simple clients, language independent, central policy. Cons: extra hop, load balancer must be highly available and scaled.

Hybrid: service mesh

Sidecars (Envoy in Istio, Linkerd) or ambient proxies resolve endpoints from the control plane and perform client-side-style load balancing, retries, mTLS and circuit breaking transparently to the app.

Operational concerns

  • Health checks: liveness, readiness; remove instances quickly but avoid flapping.
  • DNS caching and TTL: JVM and clients that cache DNS indefinitely route to dead IPs; tune TTLs. Headless Services return pod IPs for client-side balancing (gRPC needs this because HTTP/2 holds long connections).
  • Consistency: registries are eventually consistent; handle stale entries with retries and timeouts.
  • Zone awareness to reduce latency and cost, and canary or weighted routing for releases.
  • Secure registration and traffic with mTLS and ACLs.

Choosing

Kubernetes users generally rely on built-in server-side discovery plus a mesh when advanced traffic features are needed. Client-side suits high-performance, gRPC-heavy systems or non-Kubernetes environments with strong shared libraries.

  • How do health checks and TTLs keep the registry accurate?
