How do you build a recommender system, and how do collaborative filtering, content-based and matrix factorization approaches compare?

Data scientist · Gear 3 (Senior) · Recommender systems · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Collaborative filtering learns from user-item interactions, content-based methods use item and user attributes, and matrix factorization or neural embeddings combine both in latent space. Production systems use two stages, candidate retrieval then ranking, handle cold start with content features, and are judged offline by ranking metrics and online by A/B tests.

Explained in depth

Problem framing

Given users, items and interaction history (views, clicks, purchases, ratings), suggest items a user is likely to value. Decide the objective (clicks, conversions, watch time, long-term satisfaction, diversity) and the surface (homepage, email, search, similar items).

Implicit versus explicit feedback

Most data is implicit (views, clicks): there are no explicit negatives, and absence does not mean dislike. Use confidence weighting (ALS for implicit data), negative sampling, or pairwise loss (BPR) to rank observed items above unobserved ones. Beware position bias and popularity bias (items shown higher get more clicks); correct with inverse propensity weighting or randomised exploration.

Production architecture

  1. Candidate generation (retrieval): several sources (collaborative embeddings, recent items, popular in category, similar items) narrow millions of items to hundreds, in milliseconds.
  2. Ranking: a richer model scores candidates with user, item, context and cross features.
  3. Re-ranking: business rules, diversity, freshness, fairness, deduplication, inventory and caps.

Features and embeddings are served from a feature store; models are refreshed regularly.

model.fit(user_item_csr * 40)                       # confidence weights
ids, scores = model.recommend(user_id, user_item_csr[user_id], N=20)

Cold start

New users: popularity, onboarding questions, context (device, location, referrer), content-based, bandit exploration. New items: content embeddings, metadata, exploration boosts, similar-item priors.

Evaluation

Offline: time-based split (never random), leave-last-out per user, metrics such as Recall@K, Precision@K, MAP, NDCG@K, MRR, plus coverage, diversity, novelty, serendipity. Online: A/B tests on CTR, conversion, revenue, retention, and long-term effects. Offline gains often fail online because of logging bias, feedback loops and objective mismatch.

Responsible design

Avoid filter bubbles, popularity feedback loops, unfair exposure for providers, and manipulation; provide user controls and explanations.

Pitfalls

Random train-test splits, leakage from future interactions, optimising clicks at the cost of satisfaction, and ignoring cold start and inventory constraints.
