Explain instrumental variables and regression discontinuity as ways to identify causal effects without randomisation. What assumptions do they need?

Data scientist · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Causal inference · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

An instrumental variable affects treatment but influences the outcome only through treatment and is independent of confounders, letting two-stage least squares isolate a local effect for compliers. Regression discontinuity exploits a cutoff in assignment, comparing units just above and below it. Both rely on untestable assumptions that you probe with falsification and robustness checks.

Explained in depth

Why these methods

Observational comparisons are confounded. If you cannot randomise or measure all confounders, you can look for quasi-random variation: something that changes treatment for reasons unrelated to the outcome.

Instrumental variables (IV)

An instrument Z must satisfy:

  1. Relevance: Z strongly affects treatment T.
  2. Independence (exogeneity): Z is as good as randomly assigned, unrelated to unobserved confounders.
  3. Exclusion restriction: Z affects outcome Y only through T.
  4. Monotonicity (for the LATE interpretation): Z pushes everyone's treatment in the same direction (no "defiers").

Examples: randomised encouragement (a random email nudging adoption of a feature, used to estimate the effect of adoption, since some people ignore the nudge), draft lottery numbers for military service, distance to a college, random assignment of judges, weather shocks.

Estimator: two-stage least squares (2SLS). Stage 1 regress T on Z (and covariates); Stage 2 regress Y on fitted T. With one binary instrument it equals the Wald estimator: (effect of Z on Y) / (effect of Z on T). The result is the local average treatment effect (LATE): the effect for compliers, those whose treatment status is changed by the instrument, not for everyone.

from linearmodels.iv import IV2SLS
res = IV2SLS.from_formula("y ~ 1 + age + [adopt ~ nudge]", data=df).fit(cov_type="robust")
print(res.summary); print(res.first_stage)

Diagnostics: first-stage F statistic (rule of thumb above 10; weak instruments give biased, high-variance estimates), overidentification tests (Sargan or Hansen) with multiple instruments, and reasoned arguments for exclusion (cannot be tested directly). Confidence intervals can be wide; that is the price of identification.

Regression discontinuity (RD)

Treatment is assigned by whether a running variable crosses a cutoff (scholarship if test score is at least 80, credit approval at a score threshold, a policy for age 65, a loyalty tier at spend 1,000). Units just below and just above are very similar, so the jump in outcomes at the cutoff estimates the causal effect locally at the threshold.

  • Sharp RD: treatment is a deterministic function of the running variable.
  • Fuzzy RD: the cutoff changes the probability of treatment; the estimate is the jump in outcome divided by the jump in treatment (IV with the cutoff as instrument).
  • Estimate with local linear regression on each side within a bandwidth chosen by data-driven methods (Imbens-Kalyanaraman, CCT with bias correction); use a triangular kernel; report robustness to bandwidth and polynomial order.

Assumptions and checks: units cannot precisely manipulate the running variable (check density continuity with a McCrary or Cattaneo density test), covariates are smooth at the cutoff (balance tests), no other policy changes at the same threshold, and placebo cutoffs show no jumps. Pitfalls

Using instruments with weak first stages, claiming exclusion without arguments, generalising RD effects far from the threshold, and fitting high-order polynomials over wide ranges.
