Compare cryptographic hash functions: SHA-256 versus SHA-3 and the importance of collision resistance.

Security engineer · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Cryptography · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

A cryptographic hash maps data to a fixed-size digest and must be one-way, collision resistant and sensitive to any change. SHA-256 (SHA-2 family) is widely used and secure; SHA-3 (Keccak) has a different internal design, giving diversity and resistance to length-extension attacks. MD5 and SHA-1 are broken because attackers can find collisions.

Explained in depth

Properties

  • Preimage resistance: given a digest, you cannot find an input that produces it.
  • Second-preimage resistance: given one input, you cannot find another with the same digest.
  • Collision resistance: you cannot find any two inputs with the same digest. By the birthday bound, an n-bit hash offers only about n/2 bits of collision security, so SHA-256 gives about 128 bits.

SHA-3 was chosen as a backup in case SHA-2 weaknesses emerge, not because SHA-2 is broken. Use SHA-256 or SHA-512 by default; BLAKE2 and BLAKE3 are fast alternatives.

Broken hashes

MD5 and SHA-1 have practical collision attacks (a SHA-1 collision was published in 2017), so attackers can craft two different documents or certificates with the same hash. Do not use them for signatures, certificates or integrity against attackers.

Important uses and limits

  • A bare hash gives integrity against accidents, not authenticity: anyone can recompute it. For authenticity use HMAC or a digital signature.
  • Never use a fast hash such as SHA-256 alone to store passwords; use a slow password hashing function (Argon2id, bcrypt).
  • Collision resistance matters for digital signatures and certificates, because a signed hash stands for the whole document.
