How do you secure a dedicated percentage of sprint capacity (e.g. 20 percent) for ongoing technical debt?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Strategy · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Secure sustained tech debt capacity by making debt visible and tied to business outcomes: inventory and quantify it with cost of delay and risk, translate it into delivery speed, incident and security impact, propose a standing allocation such as 15 to 20 percent agreed with product, track results with metrics, and show wins so the agreement holds under pressure.

Explained in depth

Reframe technical debt

Debt is not "code we dislike". Define it as deliberate or accidental design and implementation shortcuts that increase the cost of future change or the risk of failure. Like financial debt, it carries interest (slower delivery, bugs, incidents, onboarding time) and sometimes principal (the cost to fix). Some debt is wise (shipping an MVP). Unmanaged debt compounds. The goal is a conscious, sustainable policy, not zero debt.

Build the evidence

  1. Create a debt register: list items with description, affected systems, category (code quality, architecture, test gaps, infrastructure, security, dependencies, documentation), owner, estimated effort, and the impact it causes. Gather input from engineers, SRE and support. Use tools to help: SonarQube technical debt estimates, CodeScene hotspots (change frequency times complexity), dependency age reports, DORA metrics, incident data.
  2. Quantify interest in business terms:
  • Delivery drag: extra days per feature in a fragile module ("changes to billing take 3x longer; 40 percent of its PRs need hotfixes").
  • Incidents and downtime: number, duration and customer impact traced to specific debt; revenue or SLA credits lost.
  • Security and compliance exposure: unsupported versions, unpatched dependencies, audit findings.
  • Support and operational cost: manual deploys, toil hours per week, on-call pages.
  • Onboarding and attrition: time to productivity, developer survey results (engineers citing tooling and debt as top frustration).
  • Opportunity cost: features that cannot be built until a refactor is done.

Example: "Manual releases cost 6 engineer-hours per week and cause 1 incident per quarter; automating takes 3 weeks and pays back in 5 months."

  1. Prioritise with a simple model: impact (interest cost and risk) x likelihood / effort, or a 2x2 (high-impact low-effort first; high-impact high-effort planned as projects). Consider blast radius, proximity to upcoming roadmap work (fix debt in areas you are about to change), and compounding.

Propose a policy

  • Standing capacity allocation (commonly 15 to 25 percent per sprint or a rotating quarter slice) agreed with product and leadership as part of planning, like paying a mortgage. Frame it as protecting future velocity and reliability, supported by data.
  • Embed debt in feature work (the "boy scout rule" and refactoring as part of delivery) for small items, with dedicated projects for larger ones (platform upgrades, service decomposition).
  • Alternatives: Error budget policy (when SLOs are breached, reliability and debt work takes priority), debt sprints/fix-it weeks quarterly, or a dedicated platform team for shared debt.
  • Define "debt" work types and acceptance criteria with outcome metrics (build time, change failure rate, incidents), not just refactor tasks.
  • Write down the agreement (team charter or planning rules) and review it quarterly with data.

Make it stick

  1. Track and report outcomes: before and after metrics (lead time, defect rate, CI time, incident count, cloud cost) and share wins in sprint reviews and updates ("Upgrading the framework reduced build time from 25 to 9 minutes and cut flaky tests by 60 percent").
  2. Make debt visible in planning: put items on the same backlog with product, show a debt burndown or health score, and include debt KRs in OKRs.
  3. Tie to business risk: security debt linked to audit or customer requirements, reliability debt to churn or SLA penalties.
  4. Negotiate trade-offs honestly: when deadlines require taking on debt intentionally, record it (ticket with owner, expected payoff date, "debt acceptance" note) and schedule repayment. Track "debt incurred versus repaid".
  5. Protect the allocation: when pressure rises, make trade-offs explicit with leadership ("Cutting the 20 percent for two sprints increases the risk of X; if we do, we repay in the next quarter"). Avoid silent erosion.
  6. Build alliances: partner with product, SRE and security who benefit; share the story with executives; use incidents as teaching moments (postmortem actions linked to debt).

Pitfalls

Presenting debt as engineers' preference, huge rewrites with unclear value, no measurement of results, fixing debt in rarely changed code, and letting the allocation vanish after the first deadline.

  • What do you do when a launch deadline erodes the agreed 20 percent allocation?
