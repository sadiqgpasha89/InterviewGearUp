How do you handle a brilliant jerk whose high technical output is paired with toxic team behavior?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · People · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Treat a brilliant jerk as a leadership problem: name specific behaviours and their impact, give direct feedback with clear expectations and a timeline, coach and support change, document consistently, involve HR, apply consequences if behaviour persists, and be willing to part ways. Toxic high performers drive away others and cost more than their output is worth.

Explained in depth

Why it must be addressed

A "brilliant jerk" produces strong technical output but undermines colleagues through disrespect, belittling code reviews, credit-taking, blame, hoarding information, aggression, or exclusion. Research and experience show that toxic behaviour reduces team performance, drives away good people (especially those from underrepresented groups), suppresses speaking up, and increases turnover and cost. Tolerating it signals that results trump values, harming culture and psychological safety. It may also create legal and HR risk (harassment, discrimination, hostile work environment).

Step 1: gather facts

Observe and collect specific examples, dates, and impact: code review comments, meeting behaviour, chat messages, feedback from peers, skip-levels, exit interviews, and 360 data. Distinguish direct, high-standards communication from disrespect and aggression. Consult HR early for policy and guidance, particularly if protected characteristics or harassment are involved (then follow formal investigation procedures). Protect those who raised concerns from retaliation.

Step 2: have a direct, private conversation soon

Use the SBI model (Situation, Behaviour, Impact) and avoid labels: "In Tuesday's design review (situation), you interrupted Priya three times and said her approach was stupid (behaviour). She stopped contributing, and two teammates told me they are avoiding reviews with you (impact)." Then: state expectations clearly ("disagree with ideas, not people; give review comments that are specific and respectful; credit others' work"), ask for their perspective (listen for stress, misunderstandings or genuine awareness gaps), and agree on observable changes and a timeline (for example 30 to 60 days) with check-ins. Make it clear that technical excellence is valued but behaviour is part of the job and affects performance reviews, promotion and continued employment. Document the conversation and follow up in writing.

Step 3: support change

Offer coaching or training (communication, emotional intelligence, management skills), a mentor, clear role definition if frustration stems from ambiguity, workload adjustments if burnout contributes, and constructive channels for their high standards (for example a clear code quality guideline and a review checklist). Provide frequent feedback (weekly) and recognise improvements.

Step 4: apply consequences consistently

If behaviour continues, move to formal performance management: a documented warning or performance improvement plan with specific behavioural criteria, support, timeline and consequences, in partnership with HR. Remove or limit power and visibility meanwhile: no unilateral gatekeeping on code, no mentoring responsibilities, and no promotion or leadership roles. If there is no sustained change, part ways, even if they are hard to replace. Plan for knowledge transfer and mitigate delivery risk (reduce single points of dependence, bus factor), because that dependency is often why toxic behaviour has been tolerated.

Step 5: protect and repair the team

  • Check in privately with affected people: acknowledge their experience, thank them for raising concerns, and explain (within confidentiality limits) that action is being taken.
  • Reinforce team norms (code of conduct, review guidelines, values in promotion criteria), model respectful disagreement, and recognise people who collaborate and elevate others.
  • After resolution, communicate appropriately and avoid gossip. Rebuild trust through consistent behaviour and open channels. Run a retrospective focused on team working agreements.
  • Encourage incentives that value collaboration: include teamwork, mentoring and inclusion in performance and promotion criteria, and hire for values with structured behavioural interviews.

Handling the "but they are critical" argument

Estimate the true cost: lost productivity of colleagues, attrition and replacement costs, slower hiring, quality issues from fear of reviews, and reputational risk. Compare to their contribution and build redundancy. Many leaders report that productivity improved after a toxic top performer left (the "no brilliant jerks" principle, as in Netflix's culture memo and Sutton's work).

Pitfalls

Waiting for the next performance cycle, vague feedback ("be nicer"), exempting stars, discussing the person with peers, ignoring victims' wellbeing, and rewarding output while ignoring behaviours.

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you give feedback to a high performer who does not believe their behaviour is a problem?
  • At what point do you escalate to a performance improvement plan or termination even if the person is critical to delivery?
  • How do you repair the team's trust and morale after dealing with the issue?
