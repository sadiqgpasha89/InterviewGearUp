How do you manage former peers after being promoted to lead your existing team?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Management · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Move from peer to leader by acknowledging the change openly, meeting each person one-on-one to reset expectations and listen, clarifying your role and decision rights, staying consistent and fair, separating friendship from authority, managing conflicts of interest, seeking mentoring from your own manager, and earning trust by serving the team and following through.

Explained in depth

Why it is difficult

The change in role and power alters relationships overnight. Former peers may feel jealousy, scepticism, or relief; you may feel guilt, imposter syndrome, or a desire to stay "one of the team". Typical mistakes: trying to stay everybody's friend, avoiding hard conversations, over-correcting with excess authority, continuing to do your old job instead of leading, or favouring friends.

First weeks: reset the relationships

  1. Acknowledge the change in a team meeting: thank them, state that roles have shifted, share your intent (support the team's success, be fair, listen), and invite feedback. Ask your own manager to announce your role clearly, including responsibilities and decision rights.
  2. Hold one-to-ones with every person (a listening tour): ask about their goals, concerns, what they need from a manager, what is working and what is not, how they like feedback, and what they hope you will change or keep. Acknowledge any disappointment from candidates who also wanted the role, and discuss their growth paths privately and respectfully. Do not defend the decision; listen and commit to supporting their development.
  3. Clarify the new relationship: explain what changes (you will give feedback, make assignments, evaluate performance, handle compensation) and what stays (respect, humour, collaboration). Discuss how to handle information you can no longer share (confidential HR matters, compensation, org decisions) and be honest about what you cannot discuss.
  4. Agree on working agreements: communication, meeting rhythm, how decisions are made, and how they can challenge you.

Ongoing practices

  • Be fair and transparent: apply the same standards to everyone, including friends. Explain reasoning for assignments, promotions and priorities. Disclose and manage conflicts of interest (a close friend or family on the team) with your manager and HR as needed.
  • Give honest feedback early: continue to hold people to expectations, using specific, behaviour-based, caring feedback (SBI: situation, behaviour, impact). With friends, say "I am having this conversation as your manager because I want you to succeed." Avoid delaying hard feedback, which damages trust more than the conversation itself.
  • Adjust social boundaries: you can remain friendly, but avoid venting about the company or other team members, gossip, and intimate disclosures. Be thoughtful about out-of-work socialising so no one is excluded or feels the power imbalance (alcohol-fuelled events). Keep confidences, and be consistent.
  • Let go of your old role: delegate tasks and the technical work you used to do, move from individual contributor to enabler. Stay technical enough to be credible, but avoid overruling people with "how I used to do it". Your output is the team's output.
  • Build credibility through service: remove blockers, protect the team from noise, advocate for their needs and recognise their wins, admit mistakes, and ask for feedback regularly ("What is one thing I should do differently?").
  • Handle resentment: address it calmly and directly in private ("I sense that you might be frustrated; I would like to hear your perspective"), look for underlying needs (recognition, growth, scope), offer a development plan, and set clear expectations for professional behaviour. If undermining behaviour persists, escalate through normal performance management with documentation, and involve HR.
  • Get support: meet regularly with your manager, find a mentor who has made the transition, join a manager peer group, and take management training (feedback, delegation, performance management). Expect a learning curve and be kind to yourself.

Signals of progress

People bring problems early, disagree openly in meetings, performance conversations are productive, former peers seek your advice on growth, and team engagement and delivery metrics remain healthy.

Pitfalls

Pretending nothing changed, overcompensating with strictness, favouring friends, avoiding feedback, sharing confidential information, and continuing as the team's best coder rather than its leader.
