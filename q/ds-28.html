How does k-means clustering work, how do you choose k, and when does it fail?

Data scientist · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Unsupervised learning · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

K-means alternates between assigning points to the nearest centroid and recomputing centroids as cluster means until assignments stabilise, minimising within-cluster squared distance. Choose k with the elbow method, silhouette score, gap statistic and business interpretability. It fails with non-spherical or differently sized and dense clusters, outliers, high dimensions and unscaled features.

Explained in depth

Algorithm (Lloyd's)

  1. Choose k and initialise k centroids (k-means++ spreads them out by sampling proportional to squared distance, giving better and more stable results).
  2. Assign each point to its nearest centroid (Euclidean distance).
  3. Update each centroid to the mean of its assigned points.
  4. Repeat until assignments stop changing or the inertia improvement is tiny.

It minimises inertia (within-cluster sum of squares) but converges only to a local optimum, so run several initialisations (n_init) and keep the best. Complexity is about O(n k d) per iteration; mini-batch k-means scales to huge data.

Preprocessing

  • Scale features (standardise), because distance drives everything.
  • Handle outliers (they pull centroids), consider log transforms, remove highly correlated or irrelevant features, and apply PCA to reduce noise in high dimensions.
  • Categorical data: use k-modes or k-prototypes, or embed first.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
for k in range(2, 11):
    km = KMeans(k, n_init=20, random_state=0).fit(Z)
    print(k, round(km.inertia_), round(silhouette_score(Z, km.labels_), 3))

When it fails

  • Non-spherical or elongated clusters (rings, moons): k-means assumes convex, roughly equal-variance blobs.
  • Clusters of very different sizes or densities.
  • Outliers and noise distort centroids.
  • High dimensions: distances concentrate, so use dimensionality reduction first.
  • Unknown k and the fact that every point must belong to a cluster.
  • Sensitive to initialisation and feature scaling.

Validate and use the clusters

Profile each cluster (means, distributions of key features, size), name them, test stability, check that they relate to outcomes not used in clustering (conversion, churn), and verify they are actionable. Remember clusters are a model of the data, not discovered truth; refresh periodically and assign new customers by nearest centroid.

Pitfalls

Using unscaled features, picking k from the elbow alone, over-interpreting clusters from noisy data, and clustering on columns the business cannot act on.
