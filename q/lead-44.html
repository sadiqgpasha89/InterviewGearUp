How do you conduct annual engineering headcount planning and forecast budget allocations?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Management · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Headcount planning starts from company goals and the roadmap: estimate capacity needs by workstream, subtract current capacity adjusted for attrition, leave, ramp time and non-project load, then model hiring, contractors and alternatives, build a budget with fully loaded costs and timing, present scenarios with trade-offs to finance and leadership, and re-forecast quarterly.

Explained in depth

Inputs

  1. Strategy and roadmap: company objectives (revenue, margin targets, new products), product roadmap, platform and reliability needs, regulatory commitments, tech debt and security programmes. Translate into workstreams with estimated effort (person-months), priority and confidence.
  2. Current baseline: roster by team, role, level, location, cost, tenure; open requisitions; known leave; contractors and their end dates; historical delivery data (velocity, throughput) and the share of capacity spent on run (on-call, support, maintenance) versus change work.
  3. Constraints: budget envelope and company hiring policy, location strategy and cost bands, compliance (equal pay, immigration), onboarding capacity (how many new people each team can absorb per quarter), management span (6 to 8 engineers per manager), and manager and lead needs.

Build the capacity model

Available capacity (person-months) =
  headcount x months in period x availability factor (leave, holidays, meetings; typically 0.75 to 0.85)
  - on-call, support and maintenance load
  - ramp-up drag for new hires (for example 25 percent productivity in month 1-2, 50 percent in 3-4, 100 after 6 months)
  - expected attrition (for example 10 to 15 percent annual) plus vacancy time before backfill
Demand = sum of roadmap workstream estimates (with ranges) + operational load
Gap = Demand - Capacity

Add scenarios: base, stretch and minimal. Use ranges for estimates and apply a confidence buffer. Check skills, not only numbers: SRE, security, data, mobile, staff-level architecture, and engineering management coverage.

Costing the budget

  • Fully loaded cost per head: salary and bonus, equity, payroll taxes, benefits, equipment, software licences, cloud and tooling, recruiting fees (15 to 25 percent of salary for agencies), onboarding, management overhead, facilities or remote stipends. Use cost bands by level and location.
  • Timing matters: a hire made in Q3 costs a fraction of annual cost in the fiscal year but the full cost next year (annualisation). Model hiring lead time (time to hire 2 to 4 months plus notice periods) and ramp.
  • Include non-headcount items: contractors, consulting, training, conferences, cloud, vendor tools, recruiting events, severance if restructuring, and contingency (5 to 10 percent).

Options beyond "more people"

Consider prioritisation and scope cuts, productivity investments (CI speed, platform tooling), buying versus building, automation and AI assistance, internal moves and retraining, contractors or outsourcing for bounded work, hiring in lower-cost locations, and improving retention (replacement cost is typically 0.5 to 2x salary). Show the trade-off: "Without two SREs, we accept 99.5 percent availability and defer the observability project."

Making the case

Present a concise document to finance and leadership: goals and outcomes, capacity gap analysis, business impact of funding vs not funding (revenue enabled, risk reduced, cost avoided), scenario options with costs and risks, hiring plan by quarter with roles and levels, efficiency metrics (revenue per engineer, delivery trends, cost per feature, unit economics), and dependencies on other teams. Use data from DORA, support load, roadmap commitments, attrition. Align with HR and recruiting on feasibility and market conditions (time to fill, compensation benchmarks).

Governance and tracking

Plan annually, re-forecast quarterly (and on major changes), track headcount versus plan, time to fill, offer acceptance, regretted attrition, ramp progress, and spend versus budget. Maintain a prioritised hiring list so if budget is cut or released you can adjust quickly. Communicate decisions transparently to the team.

When budget is below the ask

Rank workstreams by value and risk, negotiate scope and timeline, phase hires (critical roles first), propose cost offsets (contractor conversion, tool consolidation, cloud savings), and document the risk acceptance by leadership for what will not be done.

Pitfalls

Planning from team wishes rather than goals, ignoring ramp time and attrition, forgetting managers and support roles, one-point estimates, hiring without onboarding capacity, and treating the plan as fixed.

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you account for attrition, ramp-up time and hiring lead times when forecasting capacity?
  • How do you justify headcount to finance and decide between hiring, contractors, tools or deprioritising work?
  • What do you do when the approved budget is lower than the plan requires?
