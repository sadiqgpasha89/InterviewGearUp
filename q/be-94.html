How does the Strangler Fig pattern allow incrementally decomposing a legacy monolith into microservices?

Backend engineer · Gear 3 (Senior) · Architecture · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

The Strangler Fig pattern incrementally replaces a legacy system by placing a routing facade in front, building new services for one capability at a time, redirecting that traffic to them, and retiring the old code gradually. Risk stays low because each slice is small, reversible and delivers value, with no big-bang rewrite.

Explained in depth

Why not a rewrite

Big-bang rewrites take years, freeze features, miss moving requirements, rediscover old bugs and often fail. The strangler fig (named after vines that grow around a tree) lets new functionality grow around the old system until the old one can be removed.

Steps

  1. Identify seams: find modules and bounded contexts with clear boundaries and business value (billing, search, notifications). Use domain-driven design, call-graph analysis, change frequency and pain points.
  2. Introduce a facade: an API gateway, reverse proxy or routing layer in front of the monolith. Initially it forwards everything unchanged.
  3. Build the new service for one slice (a vertical slice of behaviour and its data) behind the facade.
  4. Route traffic gradually: by path, feature flag, user cohort or percentage (canary), compare results (shadow traffic or dual-run), then switch fully.
  5. Remove the old code for that capability and repeat.

Data migration

The hardest part. Options:

  • Start with the new service reading from the legacy database through a view or API, to avoid early data splits.
  • Use change data capture (Debezium) or events to replicate data to the new service's own database, then make it the system of record and let the monolith call the service or consume its events.
  • Run dual writes carefully (with reconciliation) or the outbox pattern; avoid distributed transactions, and prefer sagas for cross-service flows.
  • Verify with data comparison jobs before cutover.

Protecting the new design

An anti-corruption layer (ACL) translates between legacy models and new domain models so legacy quirks do not leak. Keep contracts stable with consumer-driven tests and versioned APIs. For UI, use micro-frontends or route-level splitting.

Order of extraction

Prefer slices that are: high business value or high change rate, loosely coupled, with clear data ownership, and with manageable risk (start with an edge capability such as notifications or reports, not core transactions). Postpone deeply entangled core domains until you have practice and tooling.

Operational readiness

Before the first extraction invest in CI/CD, observability (distributed tracing, centralised logs), service templates, contract testing, and automated rollback. Measure outcomes (deployment frequency, lead time, incident rate) to prove progress.

Trade-offs

Two systems run in parallel for a long time (more operational overhead), temporary duplication and integration code, consistency challenges at the seams, and the risk of ending with a distributed monolith if boundaries are wrong. Sometimes a modular monolith is the right destination, and not every module needs to become a microservice.

Pitfalls

Extracting by technical layer instead of business capability, sharing the database indefinitely, missing owners, and never finishing the retirement of the legacy code.
