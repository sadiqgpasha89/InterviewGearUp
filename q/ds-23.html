What is the multiple comparisons problem, and how do Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg corrections work?

Data scientist · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Statistics · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Testing many hypotheses at alpha 0.05 inflates the chance of at least one false positive. Bonferroni divides alpha by the number of tests and controls the family-wise error rate conservatively; Holm does the same more powerfully step by step; Benjamini-Hochberg controls the false discovery rate and is better when screening many hypotheses.

Explained in depth

The problem

If each test has a 5 percent false-positive rate and the hypotheses are all null, the chance of at least one false positive in m tests is 1 - 0.95^m: 40 percent for 10 tests, 64 percent for 20, 92 percent for 50. Data scientists meet this when they check many metrics, many variants, many segments, many genes or features, and many model comparisons. Reporting only the "significant" ones without correction is p-hacking by accident.

Error rates

  • Family-wise error rate (FWER): probability of one or more false rejections. Strict control, for confirmatory claims.
  • False discovery rate (FDR): expected proportion of false discoveries among rejections. Less strict, more power, for exploratory screening.

Methods

MethodControlsProcedureNotes
BonferroniFWERreject if p_i < alpha / m (or multiply p-values by m)simple, valid under any dependence, very conservative for large m
Holm (step-down)FWERsort p-values ascending; compare p_(i) with alpha / (m - i + 1); stop at the first failureuniformly more powerful than Bonferroni, same guarantee
Hochberg / SidakFWERvariants for independence assumptions
Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)FDRsort p-values; find the largest k with p_(k) <= (k / m) x q; reject hypotheses 1..kvalid for independent or positively dependent tests; widely used
Benjamini-YekutieliFDRBH with a harmonic-sum penaltyany dependence
Dunnett, Tukey HSDFWERdesigned for comparisons against a control or all pairs after ANOVA
Gatekeeping / hierarchical testingFWERtest the primary metric first, then secondary only if it passeskeeps power for key metric
from statsmodels.stats.multitest import multipletests
rej, p_adj, _, _ = multipletests(pvals, alpha=0.05, method="fdr_bh")     # also "bonferroni", "holm"

Adjusted p-values can be compared with alpha directly. A q-value is the FDR analogue of the p-value: the smallest FDR at which a result would be called significant.

Choosing

  • Confirmatory decisions (is the new drug better? ship decision) and few hypotheses: Holm or Bonferroni.
  • Exploratory screening (which of 5,000 genes, 200 segments or 50 features show an effect): BH with q = 0.05 or 0.1, then validate the discoveries in follow-up tests.
  • In product experimentation: pre-specify one primary metric (no correction needed), treat guardrails with caution (you worry about false negatives there, so avoid too-strict corrections), and apply FDR or Bonferroni to many secondary metrics and segments, with clear labelling of exploratory findings.

Related issues

  • Peeking and optional stopping are a form of multiple testing over time; use sequential methods.
  • Forking paths: researcher degrees of freedom (choice of outliers rule, transformations, segments). Pre-registration and holdout replication guard against it.
  • Winner's curse: among many variants, the best-looking estimate is biased upward; shrinkage or a confirmation run gives a truer estimate.
  • Bayesian hierarchical models partially pool estimates and naturally temper extreme results.

Pitfalls

Applying Bonferroni to thousands of tests and concluding nothing is real, correcting only some of the tests you ran, and slicing data until something is significant.
