Design a real-time fraud detection system for online payments, covering data, models, latency, labels and the operational loop.

Data scientist · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · ML system design · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Combine rules, a low-latency supervised model on real-time and historical entity features, and anomaly or graph signals, scored within tens of milliseconds, with decisions to approve, step-up or decline. Handle delayed and biased labels, extreme imbalance and adversaries through feedback loops, cost-based thresholds, champion-challenger deployment, and monitoring of both fraud losses and false-decline rates.

Explained in depth

Requirements

  • Latency: score within 50 to 100 ms in the payment path; availability with a safe fallback.
  • Objective: minimise total cost = fraud losses + chargeback fees + manual review cost + lost sales from false declines + customer friction.
  • Volume: thousands of transactions per second; fraud rate typically below 0.5 percent.
  • Explainability for reviewers and regulators; adaptability to new attacks.

Architecture

  1. Event ingestion: payment request with device, IP, card, account, merchant, amount, shipping and behavioural data.
  2. Real-time feature computation: velocity counters (number of transactions per card, device, IP in the last 1 minute, 1 hour, 24 hours), distance and geo mismatches, device fingerprint reputation, account age, ratio to the customer's typical spend, email and address risk signals. Maintained in a streaming system (Kafka and Flink) with an online feature store for key-based lookups.
  3. Decision engine: rules (hard blocks, regulatory checks, allow lists) plus ML score from gradient boosted trees (fast), sometimes ensembles with neural and graph-based features (shared devices or addresses linking rings), and anomaly detectors for novel patterns.
  4. Actions: approve, decline, step-up authentication (3-D Secure, OTP), or send to manual review; thresholds vary by segment (new customers, high amounts).
  5. Feedback: chargebacks, reviewer outcomes, customer disputes, and confirmed fraud flow into labelled data.

Modelling challenges

  • Extreme imbalance: use class weights or negative downsampling (then recalibrate), PR AUC, recall at a fixed false-positive rate or review budget, expected-cost curves.
  • Label delay: chargebacks arrive 30 to 120 days later; recent data are partially labelled. Use label maturity windows, train on matured periods plus faster proxy labels (reviewer decisions, early disputes), and survival-style handling.
  • Selection bias: you never observe outcomes for declined transactions. Use randomised exploration (approve a small random sample of borderline cases, within risk limits), reject inference, and counterfactual evaluation.
  • Adversarial drift: fraudsters probe and adapt; keep features hard to game, retrain frequently, add anomaly and graph signals, monitor new patterns, and run a human-in-the-loop investigation team.
  • Leakage: features must be computed as of transaction time; test point-in-time correctness.
  • Fairness and false declines: monitor decline rates across groups and countries; friction harms genuine customers.
# cost-based threshold: decline if expected fraud loss exceeds expected cost of declining
import numpy as np
def decide(p_fraud, amount, margin=0.08, friction_cost=2.0):
    expected_loss = p_fraud * amount
    cost_decline = (1 - p_fraud) * (margin * amount + friction_cost)
    return "decline" if expected_loss > cost_decline else "approve"

Real systems use several thresholds (approve, review, step-up, decline) tuned by simulation on historical data with updated costs.

Evaluation and rollout

Offline: time-based splits, PR curves, dollar-weighted recall, savings at fixed false-positive rate, performance by segment. Online: shadow mode, then champion-challenger or A/B with strict guardrails (loss limits), measure fraud rate, approval rate, review volume, false-decline estimates (customer re-attempts, surveys), and revenue.

Operations

Monitoring dashboards (volume, score distributions, feature drift, latency, approval and decline rates), alerting for attacks (spikes), model and rule versioning, kill switches, fast-patch rules, case management tools with explanations (SHAP reasons), and regular retraining with recent data.

Pitfalls

Optimising accuracy, training only on labelled declined or reviewed samples, ignoring friction costs, and relying on static rules that fraudsters learn.

Likely follow-ups

  • How would you choose the decision threshold given the costs of fraud and false declines?
  • How do you keep the model effective when fraudsters adapt?
