What is DNS TTL (Time-To-Live) and how does DNS caching impact zero-downtime traffic switching?

DevOps / SRE / Cloud · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Networking · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

DNS TTL tells resolvers how long they may cache a record. During a traffic switch, caches keep returning the old IP until their cached copies expire, so a long TTL delays cutover and rollback. Lower the TTL well before the change, then switch, and remember that some resolvers and applications ignore or extend TTLs, so keep the old target serving until traffic drains.

Explained in depth

What TTL means

Each DNS record carries a Time-To-Live in seconds set by the authoritative server. Recursive resolvers (ISP, corporate, 8.8.8.8, browsers, operating systems, libraries) cache answers for up to that long, then re-query. Caching reduces latency and load, but it means changes propagate gradually: there is no push, each cache expires independently.

www.example.com.  300  IN  A  203.0.113.10        # TTL 300 seconds (5 minutes)

Impact on traffic switching

If TTL is 86400 (24 hours) and you change the A record at noon, some clients keep using the old IP until noon the next day. For migrations, failovers and blue-green cutovers this creates a long tail of users hitting the old target. Conversely, very low TTLs (5 to 30 seconds) cost more queries, add lookup latency and load on authoritative servers, and can raise DNS provider bills.

Zero-downtime cutover procedure

  1. Days before (at least the previous TTL duration): lower the TTL to 60 to 300 seconds. This must happen early because caches still hold the old long TTL until it expires.
  2. Prepare the new environment and verify it using direct IP tests or curl --resolve host:443:NEW_IP.
  3. Switch the record (or shift weights gradually with weighted routing); both old and new backends serve traffic during propagation, so keep the old environment running and data consistent (replicated or shared) until traffic drains.
  4. Monitor traffic on both sides, errors and latency; keep the low TTL until stable.
  5. Rollback is quick because TTL is low.
  6. After stabilisation, raise the TTL again (for example 1 hour or more) to benefit from caching.

Real-world caveats

  • Some resolvers apply minimum or maximum TTLs (a floor of 30 to 300 seconds, cap of 1 day), and some ISPs and mobile carriers ignore TTLs.
  • Clients cache beyond DNS: browsers (about 1 minute internal cache), operating systems, language runtimes (the JVM caches DNS indefinitely with a security manager or for 30 seconds otherwise; set networkaddress.cache.ttl), HTTP keep-alive and connection pools that hold connections to the old IP, and long-lived connections or gRPC channels that never re-resolve.
  • Negative caching: NXDOMAIN or NODATA answers are cached for the SOA minimum or negative TTL, so a newly created record may be invisible for a while after a failed lookup.
  • CNAME chains and each record in the chain has its own TTL; alias records (Route 53) follow the target's behaviour.
  • DNSSEC adds signature validity periods that also constrain changes.

DNS-based traffic management

Route 53, Cloudflare, NS1 and others offer weighted, latency, geolocation and health-checked failover routing, returning different answers over time. Failover speed ≈ health check detection time + TTL, so it is slower than load balancer or anycast failover; use low TTLs for failover records (30 to 60 seconds) and complement with load balancers, global accelerators or anycast for faster reaction. For canaries use weighted records at small percentages.

Operational tips

Verify propagation with dig +norecurse @ns1.example.com www.example.com (authoritative answer) and dig @8.8.8.8 (cached answer and remaining TTL), use dig +trace, check multiple resolvers, and monitor query volumes after reducing TTL. Document planned TTL values per record in IaC, and avoid forgetting to restore them afterwards.

Pitfalls

Lowering TTL only at cutover time, assuming all clients honour TTL, ignoring application-level DNS caches, and shutting down the old environment immediately after the switch.
