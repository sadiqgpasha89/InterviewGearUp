How do you introduce an Engineering Tech Radar to guide enterprise adoption of new libraries?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Strategy · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

An engineering Tech Radar classifies technologies into rings such as Adopt, Trial, Assess and Hold across quadrants like languages and frameworks, tools, platforms and techniques. Introduce it with a small working group, transparent criteria, a published and versioned radar, a light process for proposals, and regular reviews so teams get guidance without losing autonomy.

Explained in depth

Purpose

A Tech Radar (popularised by Thoughtworks) is a living map of the technologies an organisation uses or considers. It helps teams choose tools consistently, reduces fragmentation and duplicated evaluations, communicates direction (what to invest in or phase out), and supports hiring, training and support planning. It is guidance, not law: teams can choose outside it with justification.

Structure

  • Quadrants: Languages and frameworks, Tools, Platforms and infrastructure, Techniques (practices such as trunk-based development, contract testing). Customise to your needs (for example data and AI).
  • Rings:
  • Adopt: proven, recommended default; widely supported and used in production.
  • Trial: worth using on real projects with limited risk; the organisation is building experience.
  • Assess: worth exploring through spikes or proofs of concept; understand impact.
  • Hold: do not start new work with it; existing use should be maintained or migrated (deprecated, risky, superseded).

Each entry has a short rationale, owner, links to docs or ADRs, and date of last review, with movement indicators (new, moved in, moved out).

Rollout plan

  1. Sponsor and working group: include senior engineers and architects from several teams, plus security, SRE and data representatives. Define the scope, governance and cadence (for example review twice a year; radar published each half). Obtain leadership support.
  2. Inventory current usage: survey repositories, services and infrastructure (service catalogue, package scans) to list what is actually used, including versions and teams.
  3. Define criteria for placement and movement, such as: fit for business needs, maturity and community health, security posture and vulnerability history, operational readiness (monitoring, upgrades, scaling), talent availability and learning curve, integration with existing platforms, licensing and total cost, vendor viability and lock-in, performance, existing internal expertise, and exit strategy. Document ADRs for decisions on Adopt and Hold.
  4. Draft the first radar collaboratively: working group proposes initial placement, then circulate for feedback in an open review (RFC style) and finalise. Publish via a website (Zalando's open-source tech-radar, Thoughtworks' Build Your Own Radar, Backstage Tech Radar plugin, simple Markdown plus JSON), accessible to everyone.
  5. Proposal process: any engineer can propose a technology or a ring change through a lightweight template (problem, alternatives, evaluation evidence, ownership, support plan). The working group reviews within a defined time (for example two weeks) and responds with a decision and rationale. Encourage trials with exit criteria (timeboxed, owner, success metrics) before moving to Adopt.
  6. Integrate into workflows: reference the radar in architecture reviews, RFC templates, new service creation (golden paths), onboarding, hiring plans and training budgets; provide templates and platform support for Adopt items. Add automated hints (dependency checks flagging Hold libraries).
  7. Review regularly: update rings as evidence accumulates, retire stale entries, track migrations away from Hold items with owners and timelines, and communicate changes via newsletter and demos.

Avoiding rigidity

Use "golden path with freedom": Adopt is the default and best supported, but teams may deviate when justified, taking responsibility for support and security. Keep the number of Adopt items manageable. Make it transparent and participatory, not top-down, and balance consistency with experimentation (allocate time for Assess work).

Handling Hold on widely used tech

Explain the reasons (end of life, security risk, cost, better alternative), set a migration policy (no new use, sunset date, effort and funding), provide migration guides and tools, and track progress. Avoid abrupt mandates without support.

Metrics

Radar usage (page views, references in RFCs), reduction in technology sprawl, time spent evaluating tools, number of services on Hold technologies, and developer satisfaction.

Pitfalls

Architect-only decisions, a static document that becomes outdated, no rationale, forbidding all experimentation, ignoring operational costs, and no resources to migrate off Hold items.

  • How do you keep a radar from becoming a restrictive approved-list that stifles innovation?
  • How do you handle technologies already widely used that you now want to place on Hold?
