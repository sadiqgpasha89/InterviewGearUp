Compare vector distance metrics: Cosine Similarity, Dot Product, and Euclidean Distance.

ML / AI engineer · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · RAG · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Cosine similarity measures the angle between vectors and ignores magnitude, dot product combines angle and magnitude, and Euclidean distance measures straight-line separation. For unit-normalised embeddings all three give the same ranking. Use the metric the embedding model was trained with, and normalise when direction alone should matter.

Explained in depth

Definitions

For vectors a and b:

  • Dot product: a · b = Σ aᵢbᵢ = ‖a‖‖b‖cos θ. Larger is more similar. Depends on both direction and magnitudes.
  • Cosine similarity: cos θ = (a · b) / (‖a‖‖b‖), in [-1, 1]. Depends only on direction. Cosine distance = 1 − cosine similarity.
  • Euclidean (L2) distance: ‖a − b‖ = sqrt(Σ(aᵢ − bᵢ)²). Smaller is more similar; depends on position in space.

Relationship

‖a − b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2 a·b. If both vectors are unit length (‖a‖ = ‖b‖ = 1), then ‖a − b‖² = 2 − 2 a·b = 2(1 − cos θ) and a·b = cos θ. Consequently cosine, dot product and Euclidean distance produce identical rankings on normalised vectors. Many embedding models output normalised vectors (or you normalise them), so the choice is mostly about computational convenience: dot product on normalised vectors is the cheapest (a single multiply-add pass and optimised BLAS/SIMD).

import numpy as np
a, b = np.random.randn(768), np.random.randn(768)
cos = a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))
a_n, b_n = a / np.linalg.norm(a), b / np.linalg.norm(b)
assert np.isclose(a_n @ b_n, cos)
assert np.isclose(np.linalg.norm(a_n - b_n) ** 2, 2 - 2 * cos)

When they differ

  • Magnitude matters: in some models (matrix factorisation recommenders, certain two-tower models trained with dot product), vector norm encodes confidence or popularity: items with larger norms score higher for many users. Cosine would discard that signal, and dot product keeps it (and may over-recommend popular items). Maximum inner product search (MIPS) is the task there.
  • Text embeddings: magnitude usually reflects text length or frequency artefacts rather than meaning, so cosine (or normalised dot) is preferred.
  • Euclidean is natural when absolute positions and scales matter (clustering with k-means, anomaly detection on features, image descriptors like SIFT), but it suffers more from the curse of dimensionality (distances concentrate in high dimensions) and from scale differences between features.
  • Un-normalised vectors produce different rankings for the three metrics: a long vector can have a large dot product with everything.

Practical guidance

  1. Use the metric the model was trained with (model card): sentence-transformers models are typically cosine; OpenAI embeddings are normalised (cosine = dot); some models document dot product (e.g. msmarco-*-dot).
  2. Normalise embeddings at write time and use inner product in the index for speed (FAISS IndexFlatIP, HNSW with inner product).
  3. Configure the vector DB metric on index creation (metric_type: COSINE, IP, L2 in Milvus; distance: Cosine|Dot|Euclid in Qdrant; pgvector operators <=> cosine distance, <#> negative inner product, <-> L2). Mixing metrics between indexing and querying yields misleading results.
  4. Beware of score interpretation: similarity thresholds are not comparable across models; calibrate on labelled data.
  5. For quantised indexes (PQ, binary), the metric influences encoding quality; normalised vectors work best with cosine-based hamming approximations.

Related metrics

Manhattan (L1), Hamming distance for binary embeddings, Jaccard for sets, Mahalanobis for correlated features.

Pitfalls

Comparing cosine scores across different models, forgetting normalisation when using inner product, using L2 with unscaled mixed features, and assuming a higher dot product always means "more relevant" when magnitudes vary.
