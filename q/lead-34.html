How do you apply the 5 Whys root cause analysis technique without assigning personal blame?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Operations · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

The 5 Whys repeatedly asks why a problem occurred, moving from the symptom toward systemic causes. To avoid blame, phrase questions about the system and process, not people ("why did the pipeline allow it?" rather than "who did it?"), treat human error as a starting point not a root cause, and end at changes that make the failure harder to repeat.

Explained in depth

How to run it

  1. State the problem factually: "Customers could not check out for 40 minutes on Tuesday."
  2. Ask why it happened, and answer with evidence (logs, timelines), not guesses.
  3. Take each answer and ask why again, usually 3 to 6 times, until you reach causes you can change.
  4. Define actions for the deep causes, with owners and dates.

Example

  • Why did checkout fail? A database migration locked the orders table.
  • Why was it run in production at peak? The deploy had no schedule guard.
  • Why no guard? The pipeline lets any approved change deploy at any time.
  • Why was the lock not detected earlier? Staging data is far smaller than production.
  • Why? We have no production-sized test environment or migration checks.

Actions: block risky migrations in peak hours, add lock-time testing and a production-like dataset.

Staying blameless

  • Ask "how did the system allow this?" instead of "who did this?". Assume people acted reasonably with the information they had.
  • If a chain ends at "Alex made a mistake", keep going: why was it easy to make, why was it not caught, why was the impact so large?
  • Involve the people closest to the event, and share the findings openly.
  • Focus on fixes in tooling, process and design (guardrails, automation, alerts) more than reminders or training.

Limits and extensions

Real incidents usually have several contributing causes, not a single chain, so combine the technique with timelines, fishbone diagrams and a wider review. Verify each "why" with data to avoid plausible stories, and follow up that actions are completed.

Likely follow-ups

  • Why are the 5 Whys sometimes criticised as too simplistic?
  • How do you handle a team that keeps landing on one person's mistake?
  • How does it fit into a blameless postmortem?
