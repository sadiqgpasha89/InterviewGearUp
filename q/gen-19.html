Compare AVL trees and Red-Black trees: Rotations, height balance invariants, and read vs write trade-offs.

Software engineer (core) · Gear 3 (Senior) · Data structures · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Both are self-balancing binary search trees with O(log N) operations. AVL trees keep subtree heights within 1 of each other, so they are shallower and faster to search; red-black trees use looser colour rules, so inserts and deletes need fewer rotations. Pick AVL for read-heavy workloads and red-black for mixed or write-heavy ones.

Explained in depth

Invariants

  • AVL: for every node the heights of the left and right subtrees differ by at most 1. Height is at most about 1.44 log2 N.
  • Red-black: the root is black, a red node never has a red child, and every path from a node to its null leaves has the same number of black nodes. Height is at most 2 log2(N + 1).

Rebalancing

  • AVL uses four rotation cases (left-left, right-right, left-right, right-left), based on the balance factor. An insert needs at most one single or double rotation; a delete may need rotations all the way up, O(log N).
  • Red-black fixes violations mostly by recolouring, with at most two rotations on insert and three on delete. Recolouring can propagate up the tree, but the structural change stays small.

Common library maps such as Java's TreeMap and typical C++ std::map implementations use red-black trees, because they balance lookup speed against update cost. Both guarantee O(log N); the difference is a constant factor that depends on the read and write mix.

In practice, for in-memory ordered maps, B-trees or skip lists are often faster because of cache behaviour, and database indexes use B-trees to reduce disk reads.
