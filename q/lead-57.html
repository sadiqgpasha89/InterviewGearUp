How do you build a business case for investing in internal developer platforms and productivity tools?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Strategy · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Build the business case for an internal developer platform by quantifying the waste it removes: onboarding time, wait for environments, duplicated toil, incidents from inconsistent setups and slow delivery. Estimate benefits in hours and risk, cost the platform team, show phased milestones with leading metrics, pilot with a willing team, and tie outcomes to company goals.

Explained in depth

Frame the problem in business terms

Executives fund outcomes: faster delivery, lower risk, lower cost, better retention. An internal developer platform (IDP) provides self-service golden paths (project scaffolding, CI/CD, environments, observability, secrets, service catalogue) so product teams spend time on features. Start with the pain: gather evidence from developer surveys, DORA metrics, support tickets, onboarding data, and interviews.

Quantify the cost of the status quo

  • Time waiting and toil: for example 60 engineers lose 3 hours per week each on environment setup, CI troubleshooting, access requests and copy-paste pipelines. At a fully loaded cost of $100 per hour, that is about $1.1M per year of capacity.
  • Onboarding: time to first production commit is 4 weeks and could be 1 week; at 20 hires per year this recovers roughly 60 engineer-weeks.
  • Delivery speed: lead time of 10 days and deployment frequency of weekly versus target daily; delayed features carry revenue impact.
  • Incidents and risk: outages from misconfigurations, security findings from inconsistent pipelines, audit costs, and compliance rework.
  • Cloud and tooling duplication: multiple CI systems, snowflake infrastructure, and idle environments.
  • Retention and morale: frustration with tooling contributes to attrition; replacement cost 0.5 to 2x salary.

Use conservative assumptions (apply a realisation factor of 30 to 50 percent to hours saved because time does not convert perfectly into output), show ranges, and cite benchmarks (DORA, Gartner, industry case studies) with caution. Distinguish hard savings (cloud, licences) from capacity gains (time redirected to roadmap) and risk reduction (expected loss avoided).

Cost and options

Estimate platform team size (for example 5 to 8 engineers plus a product manager for 100 to 200 developers), tooling and cloud, migration effort, training, and ongoing support. Compare build, buy, or assemble:

OptionProsCons
Build customtailored fit, controlhigh cost, maintenance, slow
Open source (Backstage, Crossplane, Argo CD, Terraform modules, Port)flexibility, communityintegration effort, requires team capacity
Commercial IDP / PaaS (Humanitec, Port, Cortex, OpsLevel, Qovery, Heroku-style, cloud-native)faster start, supportlicensing cost, lock-in, fit

Treat the platform as a product: users are developers, with a product manager, roadmap, SLAs, feedback loops and a thinnest viable platform approach (start with the biggest pain, such as a service template plus CI/CD golden path, then add features).

Plan and milestones

  1. Phase 0 (4 to 6 weeks): discovery, baseline metrics, select a pilot team, define success criteria.
  2. Phase 1 (one quarter): deliver the first golden path and service catalogue; migrate 2 to 3 pilot teams; measure.
  3. Phase 2: expand to more teams, add self-service infrastructure, observability and security guardrails; deprecate legacy paths.
  4. Phase 3: optimise, add cost visibility, extend to data and ML workflows.

Each phase has exit criteria and a go or no-go review, limiting risk.

Metrics and ROI

  • Leading: adoption rate (percentage of services on golden paths), time to create a service, time to first deploy for new hires, number of self-service requests, support ticket reduction, developer satisfaction and NPS, platform availability.
  • Lagging: DORA metrics, change failure rate, incident counts, security findings, cloud cost per service, time spent on toil (survey), retention.
  • ROI = (annual benefits − annual cost) / annual cost, with payback period. Present a base, conservative and optimistic case. Example: cost $1.6M per year, benefit $2.8M at 40 percent realisation, payback 9 months. Include qualitative benefits such as consistency, compliance, and faster response to security patches.

Gain sponsorship and adoption

Engage engineering leaders and product partners early, align with company OKRs (faster time to market, reliability, security posture, cost). Co-design with developers, make the platform optional but compelling (paved road, not a forced gate) and mandate only where needed (security controls), provide migration help, documentation and champions in each team, and publish success stories and metrics. Avoid "build it and they will come".

Risks and mitigations

Over-engineering, low adoption, platform team becoming a bottleneck, vendor lock-in, and unrealistic expectations. Mitigate with pilots, product management, clear scope, API-first design, and regular user feedback.

Pitfalls

Inflated productivity numbers, no baseline, building a platform without users, ignoring ongoing ownership cost, and measuring only platform outputs rather than developer outcomes.

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you quantify developer productivity gains credibly without inflating hours saved?
  • How do you decide between building a platform team, buying a product and adopting open source such as Backstage?
  • How do you drive adoption so the platform is not an unused mandate?
