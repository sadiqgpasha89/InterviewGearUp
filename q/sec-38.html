What is the Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP) and how does Just-in-Time (JIT) access enforcement work?

Security engineer · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · IAM · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Least privilege grants identities only the permissions needed for their tasks, for the shortest time and smallest scope. Just-in-time access removes standing privilege, with users requesting elevation that is approved, time-boxed and logged, then automatically revoked. Together they shrink blast radius and the window in which stolen credentials are useful.

Explained in depth

Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP)

Every user, service, process and application receives the minimum rights necessary to do its job. Dimensions:

  • Scope: which resources (one bucket, not all storage).
  • Actions: read versus write versus delete versus administer.
  • Time: permanent versus temporary.
  • Context: from managed devices, with MFA, from allowed networks.

Benefits: limits blast radius of compromised credentials and insider mistakes, reduces attack surface, supports compliance (SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI-DSS, NIST 800-53 AC-6), eases investigation, and contains malware and ransomware spread.

Applying PoLP

  1. Start from deny; grant explicit permissions through roles and groups, not individuals.
  2. Separate duties and accounts: standard daily account and a separate admin account; separate roles for build, deploy and runtime.
  3. Right-size using evidence: analyse what permissions are actually used (AWS IAM Access Analyzer policy generation from CloudTrail, last accessed data, GCP IAM Recommender, Azure PIM and access reviews, Kubernetes audit2rbac), then remove unused rights; start with monitor-only and tighten iteratively.
  4. Scope with conditions and resource-level policies: tags, source IP or VPC, MFA required, session duration, permission boundaries, SCPs.
  5. Workloads and machine identities: dedicated service accounts per workload, short-lived credentials via workload identity (IRSA, GCP Workload Identity, managed identities, SPIFFE), no shared or long-lived keys, scoped tokens (OAuth scopes), network policies and egress limits.
  6. Data-level least privilege: row and column permissions, masking, read-only replicas for analytics.
  7. Lifecycle management: joiner, mover, leaver automation, access recertification (quarterly reviews), expire contractor and temporary access.
  8. Local privilege: remove admin rights from endpoints, use application allow-listing, sudo with specific commands, no-new-privileges and dropped capabilities for containers.

Just-in-Time (JIT) access

Standing privilege (permanent admin) is high risk because attackers who steal the credential get constant power. JIT grants elevated access only when needed:

  1. User requests a role or resource with justification and ticket reference (incident ID, change request) and desired duration.
  2. Approval: automatic by policy for low risk, or by a manager, resource owner or peer for high risk; optional MFA step-up and device posture checks.
  3. System provisions temporary entitlement (add to group, assume role with short-lived credentials, issue a time-bound certificate or database credential).
  4. Session recording and logging of privileged activity.
  5. Automatic revocation on expiry; alerts when access is used outside the window or not revoked.

Tools: Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management (PIM), AWS IAM Identity Center with temporary permission sets and Systems Manager Session Manager, Teleport, CyberArk, BeyondTrust, Delinea, HashiCorp Boundary and Vault dynamic credentials, GCP PAM and IAM Conditions with expiry, Kubernetes via short-lived kubeconfig or Just-in-time RBAC bindings, ChatOps approvals (Slack workflows), Opal, ConductorOne.

Break-glass

Emergency accounts with high privilege stored securely (sealed credentials, hardware tokens), excluded from conditional access outages, monitored with immediate alerts, tested regularly, and credentials rotated after each use. They are an exception path, not a daily route.

Measuring success

Percentage of identities with standing admin, count of unused permissions, mean elevation duration, number of break-glass uses, time to revoke leavers, findings from access reviews, and policy violations detected.

Common obstacles and answers

Developer friction (provide self-service JIT with fast approval and good defaults), unclear needs (use logs to generate minimal policies), legacy systems with all-or-nothing rights (wrap with proxies, vault credentials, session brokering), and service accounts with excessive rights (replace with scoped workload identities).

Pitfalls

Granting broad roles "temporarily" and never revoking, approval fatigue leading to rubber-stamping, JIT without logging, shared admin accounts, and least privilege applied to people while pipelines and bots run as owner.
