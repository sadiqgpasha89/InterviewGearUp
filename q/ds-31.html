How do you estimate a causal effect from observational data using propensity scores?

Data scientist · Gear 3 (Senior) · Causal inference · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Model the probability of treatment given confounders, then use that propensity score to match, weight (IPW) or stratify so treated and control groups become comparable. Check overlap and covariate balance, estimate the effect with doubly robust methods where possible, and be explicit that unmeasured confounding can still bias the result.

Explained in depth

The problem

When treatment is not randomised (users who chose a feature, patients who received a drug), treated and untreated groups differ in confounders that also affect the outcome. A naive difference in means mixes the treatment effect with that selection bias. Causal inference tries to recover a quantity such as the ATE (average treatment effect) or ATT (effect on the treated) under explicit assumptions.

Assumptions

  1. Conditional ignorability (no unmeasured confounding): given observed covariates X, treatment is as good as random.
  2. Positivity (overlap): every kind of unit has a non-zero chance of both treatment and control (0 < e(X) < 1).
  3. SUTVA / consistency: no interference between units, and a well-defined treatment.

Only the first is untestable, and it is usually the weakest.

Propensity score

The propensity score e(X) = P(T = 1 | X) is the probability of receiving treatment given covariates (Rosenbaum and Rubin). Conditioning on this single number balances all of X. Estimate it with logistic regression or gradient boosting (calibrated), using pre-treatment covariates only. Include variables that affect the outcome, even if they affect treatment weakly; do not include post-treatment variables or colliders.

Using the score

Matchingpair each treated unit with nearest controls by score (with a caliper)intuitive; discards data; use with replacement carefully

e = np.clip(e, 0.02, 0.98)                               # trimming for stability
w = t / e + (1 - t) / (1 - e)

Diagnostics (do not skip)

  • Overlap: plot propensity distributions for both groups; restrict to the region of common support when overlap is poor.
  • Covariate balance after weighting or matching: standardised mean differences below 0.1 for each covariate; compare before and after (a "love plot").
  • Check effective sample size after weighting.
  • Placebo and negative-control outcomes that treatment should not affect.
  • Sensitivity analysis (E-values, Rosenbaum bounds): how strong would an unmeasured confounder need to be to explain away the effect?

Interpretation and limits

Propensity methods adjust only for observed confounders. They do not fix unmeasured confounding, measurement error, or selection into the sample. Report the estimand (ATE, ATT, or overlap-weighted), assumptions, and uncertainty (bootstrap the whole pipeline including the propensity model). Where possible, prefer a randomised experiment, or natural experiments (difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, instrumental variables).

Pitfalls

Conditioning on mediators or colliders, ignoring positivity violations, treating balance on observed variables as proof of unconfoundedness, and using the propensity model's predictive accuracy as a quality measure (good balance matters, not high AUC).
