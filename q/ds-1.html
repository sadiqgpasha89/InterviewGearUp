When are the mean, median and mode misleading, and how do you choose which summary statistic to report?

Data scientist · Gear 1 (Junior) · Statistics · Difficulty: easy · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

The mean is sensitive to outliers and skew, the median is robust but ignores magnitude of the tails, and the mode is useful for categorical or multimodal data. Choose by the distribution shape and the decision: income and latency are skewed so report the median and percentiles, while totals and budgets need the mean.

Explained in depth

The three measures

  • Mean: the sum divided by the count. It uses every value, so it is efficient for roughly symmetric data, and it is the right quantity when you care about totals (revenue per user times users equals revenue).
  • Median: the middle value after sorting. It is robust: up to half of the data can be extreme without moving it. It describes the "typical" observation.
  • Mode: the most frequent value. It is the only one that works for nominal categories (most common browser) and it reveals multimodal shapes.

When each misleads

SituationProblemBetter choice
Right-skewed data (income, house prices, session length, API latency)a few huge values pull the mean up so "the average" describes almost nobodymedian plus percentiles (p90, p95, p99)
Outliers from errors (a sensor reporting 99999)the mean is dragged by bad datafix or flag the error, then use the median or a trimmed mean
Bimodal data (two customer segments)mean and median can fall in an empty valley between the peaksreport both modes or split by segment
Ratios and rates (conversion per session)averaging per-group averages ignores group sizes (Simpson's paradox)compute the weighted overall rate, then segment
Heavy tails (Pareto, revenue per user)the sample mean is unstable, because it varies a lot between samplesmedian, log transform, or bootstrap intervals
Ordinal data (ratings 1 to 5)the distance between categories is not equalmedian and the full distribution

A small example

Five salaries: 40k, 42k, 45k, 48k, 500k. Mean = 135k (nobody earns near that). Median = 45k (representative). The mean is still correct for budgeting the total payroll (675k).

import pandas as pd
s = pd.Series([40, 42, 45, 48, 500])
print(s.mean(), s.median(), s.mode().tolist())
print(s.describe(percentiles=[.5, .9, .99]))
from scipy import stats
print(stats.trim_mean(s, 0.2))    # drops 20% from each tail: 45

How to decide in practice

  1. Plot first: histogram or density, box plot, ECDF. Shape decides the summary.
  2. Ask what the decision needs: typical experience (median), total or expected value (mean), most common case (mode), worst cases (high percentiles).
  3. Report spread with the centre: standard deviation for symmetric data, interquartile range or percentiles for skewed data.
  4. For comparisons (A/B test of revenue), the mean answers business questions about totals, but use bootstrap or robust methods because heavy tails make standard errors unreliable.
  5. State sample size and any exclusions so readers can judge reliability.

Interview pitfalls

Saying "the mean is always wrong for skewed data" (it is the right target for totals), forgetting that the median of group medians is not the overall median, and skipping the plot.

Likely follow-ups

  • Why is the median latency usually reported together with p95 and p99?
  • What summary would you use for a bimodal distribution?
  • How does a trimmed mean or winsorising compare with the median?
