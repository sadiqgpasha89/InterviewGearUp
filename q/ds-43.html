What common problems invalidate an A/B test, such as sample ratio mismatch, novelty effects, and network interference, and how do you detect them?

Data scientist · Gear 3 (Senior) · Experimentation · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Validity threats include sample ratio mismatch from broken assignment or logging, novelty and primacy effects that fade, interference where treated users affect controls, selection from triggering errors, peeking, and Simpson's paradox across segments. Detect them with SRM chi-square checks, A/A tests, time-sliced effects, guardrail metrics, and designs such as cluster or switchback randomisation.

Explained in depth

1. Sample ratio mismatch (SRM)

If you planned a 50/50 split but observed 50,500 versus 49,500 users, a chi-square goodness-of-fit test may say this is highly unlikely by chance. SRM means assignment or data collection is biased: redirects dropping users in one arm, bot filtering that differs by arm, a variant that crashes before logging, a rollout bug, or users deduplicated differently. Results from an experiment with SRM are untrustworthy because the groups are no longer comparable. Always run the check automatically (p below 0.001 triggers an alert) and investigate before interpreting.

from scipy.stats import chisquare
obs, exp = [50500, 49500], [50000, 50000]
print(chisquare(obs, exp).pvalue)

2. Novelty and primacy effects

Users click new things because they are new (novelty), or resist change at first (primacy). Initial effects then drift. Detect by plotting effect by day or by exposure cohort (new versus returning users), running long enough for the effect to stabilise, and using a holdback (a small long-term control) for lasting impact.

3. Interference (SUTVA violations)

Treated users influence controls: social networks (sharing, messaging), marketplaces (a better ranking for buyers in treatment consumes inventory that control buyers would have bought), shared resources (ads budgets, driver supply, cache), learning algorithms trained on pooled data. Naive estimates are biased, usually overstating the effect. Remedies: cluster randomisation (by community, city, market), switchback or time-based randomisation (alternate treatment over time blocks within a region), geo experiments, ego-cluster designs, budget-split ads experiments, and modelling spillovers.

4. Selection and triggering errors

Analysing only users who "saw" a feature is valid only if the trigger condition is symmetric across arms and logged independent of treatment. Otherwise you introduce bias. Intent-to-treat analysis on all assigned users is the safe baseline.

5. Statistical issues

  • Peeking and optional stopping inflate false positives.
  • Multiple comparisons across metrics, segments and variants.
  • Variance underestimation from analysing at the wrong unit (sessions vs users) or heavy tails.
  • Underpowered tests leading to overinterpretation of noise; winner's curse.
  • Simpson's paradox: pooled results contradict segment results because of uneven assignment across strata; ensure randomisation balance and analyse stratified.

6. Data and implementation problems

Logging differences between arms, bots and internal traffic, cookie churn and cross-device users counted twice, time zones, experiment overlap and interactions with other tests, caching and CDNs serving the wrong variant, ramp changes mid-test (changing allocation over time creates Simpson's issues), and metric definition changes.

Defences

  • A/A tests (no difference) to validate the platform, variance estimates, and false-positive rates.
  • Pre-registration of hypothesis, metric, duration and decision rule.
  • Guardrail metrics (latency, errors, unsubscribe, revenue) and segment checks for harm.
  • Balance checks on pre-experiment covariates.
  • Consistent ramp plans and exposure logging; keep experiments' analysis code reproducible.
  • Replicate surprising results; be sceptical of twice-too-good effects (Twyman's law).

Pitfalls

Ignoring SRM because "it's only 1 percent off", trusting results on a marketplace with user-level randomisation, calling a result after the first big day, and slicing into many segments until one wins.

Likely follow-ups

  • How would you design an experiment on a two-sided marketplace where treatment affects supply and demand?
  • How do you distinguish a novelty effect from a real long-term lift?
