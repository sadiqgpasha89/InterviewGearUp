How does mutual TLS (mTLS) provide service-to-service authentication and encryption in a zero-trust mesh?

Backend engineer · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Security · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

In mutual TLS both sides present certificates and verify each other, so a service proves who it is as well as the server. This gives encrypted traffic and a cryptographic workload identity for authorisation, without passwords or tokens. In a service mesh, sidecars or proxies handle certificate issuance and rotation automatically.

Explained in depth

Plain TLS versus mutual TLS

In ordinary TLS only the server presents a certificate, and the client checks it. In mTLS the server also requests a client certificate and verifies that it is signed by a trusted certificate authority (CA). Both ends prove their identity during the handshake, and all traffic is encrypted.

Why it fits zero trust

Zero trust assumes the network is hostile, so being inside the cluster is not proof of identity. With mTLS:

  • A service authenticates the caller's identity (for example a SPIFFE ID) and can apply policy: only the checkout service may call the payment service.
  • Traffic cannot be read or altered on the wire, and impersonation requires a private key signed by the CA.

In a service mesh (Istio, Linkerd)

  • A control plane acts as a CA and issues short-lived workload certificates (hours) to each sidecar.
  • Sidecars perform the handshake, so application code stays unchanged.
  • Rotation is automatic; no one manages long-lived keys.
  • Authorisation policies match on the verified identity.

Operational points

  • Keep the CA root protected and plan its rotation.
  • Monitor certificate expiry and clock skew; expired certificates cause outages.
  • mTLS proves which service is calling, not that the request is permitted; combine with authorisation rules, and still authenticate end users separately (for example with JWTs).
  • The handshake adds CPU and latency, mitigated by connection reuse.
