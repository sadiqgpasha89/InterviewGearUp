What is survival analysis and why is it better than plain classification for modelling customer churn or time-to-event outcomes?

Data scientist · Gear 3 (Senior) · Survival analysis · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Survival analysis models time until an event while handling censored customers who have not yet churned. Kaplan-Meier estimates survival curves, Cox proportional hazards relates covariates to the hazard rate, and tree or deep survival models add flexibility. It uses all partial histories, so avoids the bias of labelling still-active users as non-churners.

Explained in depth

The problem with simple classification

A churn classifier asks "did the customer churn within 90 days?". Customers observed for only 30 days cannot be labelled properly: dropping them wastes data, labelling them "not churned" biases rates downward, and the model ignores when churn happens. Survival analysis models time to event and uses information from everyone, including those who have not experienced the event.

Core concepts

  • Event: churn, default, failure, conversion, recovery.
  • Censoring: for some units the event has not occurred by the end of observation (right-censoring) or they left the study; we only know the duration is at least t. Valid methods assume non-informative censoring (censoring unrelated to the event risk).
  • Survival function S(t) = P(T > t): probability still "alive" at time t.
  • Hazard h(t): instantaneous event rate at t among those who survived to t.
  • Cumulative hazard H(t); S(t) = exp(-H(t)).

Methods

MethodUse
Kaplan-Meier estimatornon-parametric survival curve by group; compare with the log-rank test
Cox proportional hazardssemi-parametric regression: h(t given x) = h0(t) exp(beta x); coefficients give hazard ratios (exp(beta) = 1.3 means 30 percent higher hazard)
Parametric models (Weibull, log-normal, AFT)specify the distribution; extrapolate beyond data and predict time directly
Random survival forests, gradient-boosted survival (XGBoost AFT/Cox), DeepSurv, discrete-time modelsnon-linear effects and interactions
Discrete-time hazard modelsexpand each customer into customer-period rows and fit a logistic or GBDT model; simple, flexible, supports time-varying features
from lifelines import KaplanMeierFitter, CoxPHFitter
km = KaplanMeierFitter().fit(df["tenure_days"], event_observed=df["churned"])
cph = CoxPHFitter(penalizer=0.1).fit(df[["tenure_days", "churned", "plan", "tickets_90d", "age"]],
                                     duration_col="tenure_days", event_col="churned")
cph.print_summary(); cph.check_assumptions(df)

Assumption checks and extensions

  • Proportional hazards: hazard ratios constant over time; check Schoenfeld residuals and log-log plots; fix with stratification, time-varying coefficients, or an alternative model.
  • Time-varying covariates (usage this month, plan changes) via a long data format with start and stop times.
  • Competing risks (voluntary versus involuntary churn, death versus transplant): use cause-specific hazards or Fine-Gray models; Kaplan-Meier on one cause overstates its probability.
  • Left truncation (late entry), recurrent events, and cure fractions (some never churn).
  • Evaluate with concordance index (C-index), time-dependent AUC, calibration of predicted survival at fixed horizons, and the integrated Brier score; split by time to mimic deployment.

Using the output

Predict risk at 30, 90, 180 days, identify high-hazard segments and drivers, estimate customer lifetime value from expected survival times margin, and time interventions. For causal conclusions about retention offers, use experiments or uplift models, not hazard ratios from observational data.

Pitfalls

Treating censored customers as non-events, using random splits when time matters, ignoring competing risks, and reading hazard ratios as causal effects.
