How do you establish Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 service tiers with clear SLA and SLO commitments?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Operations · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Define service tiers by business impact (revenue, customers, data sensitivity, dependencies), then attach each tier an availability SLO, latency targets, support hours, response times, on-call level, testing and DR requirements. SLAs are customer-facing contracts with consequences, set looser than internal SLOs, and error budgets guide the trade-off between reliability work and feature delivery.

Explained in depth

Vocabulary

  • SLI (service level indicator): a measured quantity such as the proportion of successful requests, p99 latency, freshness, or throughput.
  • SLO (objective): an internal target for an SLI over a window (for example 99.9 percent of requests succeed over 30 days).
  • SLA (agreement): an external contract with penalties (credits, termination rights). Set the SLA below the SLO so you have room to react before breaching it.
  • Error budget: 100 percent minus the SLO. At 99.9 percent monthly, the budget is about 43 minutes of downtime or the equivalent in failed requests.

Designing tiers

  1. Inventory services (service catalogue such as Backstage or OpsLevel) with owner, dependencies, users, data classification and revenue impact.
  2. Classify by business criticality, with a few clear questions: Does an outage stop revenue or safety? How many customers are affected? Is there a regulatory or contractual obligation? Is there a workaround? How quickly does impact grow?

  1. Choose SLIs that reflect user experience (request success rate at the load balancer, latency measured at the edge, data freshness, job completion), not just CPU or host health. Use realistic targets based on historical performance and what customers need. Each additional nine costs roughly an order of magnitude more effort and money, so choose the cheapest target that satisfies the business. Dependencies cap reliability: a service cannot exceed the availability of its critical hard dependencies unless it degrades gracefully.
  2. Error budget policy: agree with product and leadership in writing: when the budget is healthy, ship features; when it is burning too fast (multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts such as 2 percent budget in 1 hour), freeze risky launches and prioritise reliability work until it recovers. Budgets make the reliability versus velocity trade-off objective and shared.
  3. Alert on SLO burn, not on every symptom, to cut noise and page only on user impact.
  4. Document SLAs with measurement method, exclusions (scheduled maintenance, customer-caused issues), credits, and support commitments, reviewed with legal and sales. Do not promise a customer SLA that exceeds your internal capability.
  5. Review cadence: quarterly review of tier assignments, SLO attainment, incident trends and budget usage; adjust targets and tiers as the business changes. Publish a dashboard and status page.

Handling dependencies

A Tier 1 service must not hard-depend on a Tier 3 one. Options: raise the dependency's tier and invest, add caching, fallbacks, timeouts, circuit breakers and graceful degradation, or move the dependency out of the critical path. Include dependency SLOs in design reviews.

Rollout

Start with a few critical services, learn from measurement, then expand. Provide templates, tooling (Prometheus recording rules, Sloth, Nobl9, Datadog SLOs, Google Cloud SLO monitoring), and training.

Pitfalls

Tiering everything as Tier 1, SLOs nobody measures, setting 100 percent targets, using SLAs as marketing numbers, ignoring dependencies, and treating error budgets as punishments.
