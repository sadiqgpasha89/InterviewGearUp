How do you facilitate a blameless postmortem following a major customer-facing outage?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Operations · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

A blameless postmortem examines how a failure happened and how the system allowed it, without assigning fault to individuals. Gather a timeline with data, hold a facilitated meeting soon after resolution, focus on contributing factors and what went well, produce prioritised actions with owners and dates, and share the write-up so the whole organisation learns.

Explained in depth

Principle

People generally act sensibly with the information and tools they have. If an engineer could cause a major outage with one command, the system lacked protection. Blame makes people hide mistakes; blamelessness makes information flow and problems get fixed. It does not mean ignoring accountability for follow-up work.

Before the meeting

  1. Assign an owner to write the draft within a few days of resolution.
  2. Build a timeline from logs, alerts, chat and deploy history: detection, response steps, decisions and mitigation, with times.
  3. Collect impact: duration, customers affected, revenue or SLO consumption.

Running the meeting (30 to 60 minutes)

  • A neutral facilitator who is not the main responder.
  • Walk through the timeline and ask "what did you know at that moment, and what made that decision look reasonable?" Use "how" and "what" questions rather than "why did you".
  • Identify contributing factors (several, not one root cause): missing alerts, unclear runbooks, risky deploy process, capacity limits, communication gaps.
  • Note what went well and where luck helped.

Output

  • A written report: summary, impact, timeline, causes and contributing factors, detection and response analysis, lessons.
  • Action items that are specific, prioritised, with owners and due dates, in the normal backlog. Prefer systemic fixes (automation, guardrails, monitoring) over "be more careful".
  • Share it widely, and review completion of actions.

Pitfalls

Naming individuals, stopping at "human error", too many low-value actions, and no follow-through. Leaders set the tone: reward honest reporting.

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you keep the discussion from drifting into blaming someone?
  • How do you decide which action items are worth doing?
  • What do you do if leadership wants to know who is responsible?
