How do you delegate critical projects to tech leads without micromanaging or abdicating accountability?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Management · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Delegate outcomes, not tasks: choose the right tech lead, agree the goal, constraints, decision rights and success measures up front, match the level of delegation to the person's experience, set check-in checkpoints and escalation triggers, provide support and air cover, and review results with feedback. You stay accountable for the outcome, so monitor signals without taking over the work.

Explained in depth

The delegation trap

Micromanagement signals low trust, slows delivery, and prevents growth. Abdication (delegate and disappear) creates surprises and unowned risk. The goal is clear ownership with visible accountability, so the tech lead decides how, while you keep sight of whether.

Choose the right person and level

Assess the lead's skills, context, appetite and workload. Use a delegation ladder (Management 3.0 delegation poker or situational leadership):

  1. Tell: I decide and inform you.
  2. Sell: I decide and explain why.
  3. Consult: I ask your input, then decide.
  4. Agree: we decide together.
  5. Advise: you decide after my input.
  6. Inquire: you decide and tell me afterwards.
  7. Delegate: you decide, no need to inform.

Choose the level per decision based on risk, reversibility and the person's competence and confidence. A new lead on a critical migration may start at "advise" for architecture and "delegate" for task breakdown, moving up as trust builds.

Set up the delegation (the contract)

Hold a kickoff conversation and write it down:

  • Outcome and why: the business goal, success metrics, deadline, and what "done" and "good" look like.
  • Scope and constraints: budget, headcount, dependencies, compliance and architectural guardrails, non-negotiables (security, SLOs).
  • Decision rights: which decisions they can make alone, which need consultation (architecture council, security review, product), which need your approval (cost over X, scope change, hiring).
  • Resources and authority: people, access, air cover with stakeholders; announce the lead's role to the teams involved so authority is real.
  • Checkpoints: agree on cadence and form (weekly 1:1 status, a written update, milestone demos, design review before build, risk review at 30 percent and 70 percent). Define escalation triggers (schedule slip over a week, cost over 10 percent, security finding, key person leaving, unresolved cross-team conflict) so you hear about problems early without constant monitoring.
  • Support: ask "what do you need from me?", offer mentorship, introductions and a sounding board.

Stay informed without hovering

Use lightweight, outcome-focused signals: a shared dashboard (burn-up, risks, milestones), RFCs and design docs, demo days, a risk register, and ask coaching questions in 1:1s ("What is the riskiest assumption? What would you do if the vendor slips? What are you worried about?"). Prefer reviewing artefacts (plans, docs) over inspecting code or joining every meeting. Observe team health (morale, load) through skip-levels or team retros without undermining the lead.

When things go off track

  • Coach first: ask questions, share perspectives, and let them propose solutions. Provide feedback early and specifically.
  • Intervene proportionally: unblock via your network, add capacity, adjust scope, or pair a senior advisor. Take over only if the risk threatens the business or the lead requests it, and then communicate openly and temporarily (explain why and when you will hand back).
  • Never undermine them publicly or reverse their decisions without a conversation; if you must overrule, explain and restore ownership afterwards.

Accountability and growth

You remain accountable to your leadership for outcomes, so make sure you can explain status and risks. Hold the tech lead accountable for results and behaviours with regular feedback and a retrospective afterwards: what went well, what you would change, and how to credit the lead for success and share the lessons from setbacks. Recognise publicly and link the work to promotion evidence.

Handling "not how I would do it"

Define quality bars for outcomes and constraints, but accept different valid approaches. Ask whether the difference matters to customers, safety or maintainability; if not, let it go. Share your preferences as input, not instructions.

Pitfalls

Delegating tasks without authority, vague expectations, no checkpoints, rescuing too early, taking back the work after one stumble, and delegating only boring work.

Likely follow-ups
