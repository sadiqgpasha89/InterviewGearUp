How does k-fold cross-validation work, and when would you use stratified, grouped or time-series variants?

Data scientist · Gear 1 (Junior) · Model evaluation · Difficulty: easy · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

K-fold cross-validation splits the data into k parts, trains on k minus one and validates on the remaining part, rotating until every part has been the validation fold, then averages the scores. Use stratified folds for imbalanced classes, grouped folds for repeated entities and time-ordered folds for temporal data.

Explained in depth

Procedure

  1. Shuffle (when appropriate) and split the data into k equal folds.
  2. For each fold i: train on the other k - 1 folds, evaluate on fold i.
  3. Report the mean and standard deviation of the k scores. The mean estimates expected performance; the spread shows sensitivity to the sample.
  4. For the final model, refit on all training data with the chosen hyperparameters and evaluate once on a held-out test set.
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_validate, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
res = cross_validate(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)), X, y,
                     cv=cv, scoring=["roc_auc", "average_precision"], return_train_score=True)
print(res["test_roc_auc"].mean(), res["test_roc_auc"].std())

Choosing k

  • k = 5 or 10 are common defaults. Larger k means training sets closer to the full data (less pessimistic bias) but more computation and highly correlated folds.
  • Leave-one-out (k = n) has low bias, high variance and high cost; use only for tiny datasets.
  • Small k (2 or 3) is cheap but the training sets are much smaller than the final one, so estimates are pessimistic.
  • Repeated k-fold (several random partitions) reduces variance of the estimate.

Variants

VariantUse whenWhy
Stratified K-Foldclassification, especially imbalancedeach fold keeps the class proportions, so rare classes appear in every fold
Group K-Foldseveral rows per customer, patient, session, devicean entity must not appear in both train and validation, otherwise the model memorises the entity
Time-series split (expanding or rolling window)forecasting or any temporally ordered problemalways train on the past and validate on the future
Stratified Group K-Foldimbalance and groups togetherboth constraints
Nested cross-validationsmall data when you tune hyperparameters and also need an unbiased performance estimateinner loop tunes, outer loop evaluates; avoids optimistic bias from tuning on the evaluation folds

Pitfalls and good practice

  • Leakage: scaling, imputation, feature selection, target encoding and resampling (SMOTE) must be fitted inside each training fold, so use a Pipeline. Otherwise the scores are optimistic.
  • Do not shuffle time-ordered data. Do not oversample before splitting.
  • Using the same CV folds to tune dozens of models and then reporting that score overfits to CV; keep a final test set.
  • Pick the scoring metric that reflects the business objective, and check variance across folds.
  • Use the same fold assignments (fixed seeds) when comparing models, and use paired comparisons.

When not to use it

Very large data where a single hold-out is reliable and cheaper, or deep learning with long training times (use one validation split or a few seeds).
