When and why would you use mixed-effects or hierarchical Bayesian models instead of pooling all data or fitting separate models per group?

Data scientist · Gear 4 (Lead / Staff) · Statistical modeling · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Hierarchical models treat groups such as stores, users or schools as draws from a population, so group estimates are partially pooled: small groups borrow strength from the overall mean while large groups keep their own signal. They handle clustered, repeated-measures data correctly, give better predictions for sparse groups and avoid both the bias of complete pooling and the noise of no pooling.

Explained in depth

The three options

Imagine estimating conversion rate for 500 stores, many with few visits.

  1. Complete pooling: one global rate. Ignores real differences; biased for each store.
  2. No pooling: a separate estimate per store. Unbiased but extremely noisy for small stores (3 conversions out of 5 visits suggests 60 percent), and extreme values dominate rankings.
  3. Partial pooling (hierarchical model): store effects are drawn from a population distribution, with its mean and spread learned from the data. Estimates are a precision-weighted compromise: a store with lots of data stays near its raw rate; a store with little data is shrunk toward the overall mean. This is the empirical-Bayes and James-Stein idea, and it improves out-of-sample prediction.

Model sketch

Levels: observations within groups (customers within stores, repeated measures within users). For group j: y_ij = alpha_j + beta x_ij + eps_ij, with alpha_j ~ Normal(mu, tau^2). tau (between-group spread) is learned: if tau is small, groups look alike and shrinkage is strong; if large, groups differ and shrinkage is weak. Add random slopes (the effect of price varies by store) with a covariance between intercepts and slopes, and group-level predictors (store size, region). For binary outcomes use a hierarchical logistic model; for counts, Poisson or negative binomial.

Fixed versus random effects

  • Fixed effects: parameters of interest estimated separately, with no distribution over them (treatment effect, price coefficient).
  • Random effects: group-specific deviations assumed drawn from a distribution; we estimate their variance. Use random effects when groups are a sample from a larger population, there are many groups, and you want to generalise or predict for new groups. (In econometrics, "fixed effects" often means group dummies; be explicit about terminology.)

Why it matters

  • Correct inference: observations within a cluster are correlated; ignoring clustering understates standard errors and produces false positives (A/B tests randomised by store, repeated measures on users). Mixed models or cluster-robust standard errors fix this.
  • Better estimates for sparse groups (new products, small markets, cold-start users); sensible rankings (use posterior means rather than raw rates).
  • Multilevel structure and variance partitioning: the intraclass correlation (ICC) tells how much variation is between groups.
  • Unbalanced data are fine.
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

print(m.summary())
# Bayesian version: PyMC / Stan / brms  e.g. y ~ price + (1 + price | store)

In Bayesian form (PyMC, Stan, brms, bambi) you place priors on mu, tau and coefficients and get full posteriors, including uncertainty on group effects and predictions for new groups. Use a non-centred parameterisation to help sampling when tau is small, and check divergences, R-hat, effective sample size and posterior predictive checks.

Practical guidance

  • Start with random intercepts, add random slopes when justified by theory and data; models can fail to converge with too many random effects and few groups.
  • Few groups (under about 5 to 10) make variance estimates unreliable; consider fixed effects or informative priors.
  • Include group-level covariates to explain differences and reduce shrinkage toward a single mean.
  • Evaluate with held-out groups and held-out observations.

Pitfalls

Treating clustered observations as independent, ranking groups on raw rates, forgetting that shrinkage biases individual estimates deliberately, and overcomplicating the random-effects structure.
