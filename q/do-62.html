How do you design a high-reliability on-call rotation that prevents alert fatigue and burnout?

DevOps / SRE / Cloud · Gear 3 (Senior) · SRE · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

A sustainable on-call rotation uses enough people, clear ownership, a primary and secondary structure, humane shift lengths and handoffs, actionable low-noise alerts tied to SLOs, runbooks, compensation and recovery time, and continuous measurement of page load and toil. The goal is reliability without burning out the team.

Explained in depth

Principles

On-call exists to protect users and the business, but unmanaged it causes alert fatigue, sleep loss, attrition and slower response. Google SRE guidance: keep the interruption rate low (at most about two incidents per 12-hour shift), spend no more than about 25 to 50 percent of an SRE's time on operational work, and fix causes rather than normalise pages.

Rotation design

  • Team size: at least 6 to 8 engineers in one rotation so each person is on call roughly one week in six to eight; two people for primary and secondary (primary responds, secondary backs up and handles escalation or overflow).
  • Shift length: one week is common; for heavy services use shorter shifts or split day and night. Follow-the-sun across time zones avoids night pages for teams with global presence.
  • Handoff ritual: a documented meeting or note covering ongoing incidents, risky changes, flaky alerts and tickets opened during the shift.
  • Fairness and flexibility: swap and override support in the paging tool (PagerDuty, Opsgenie, Grafana OnCall), vacation and holiday coverage, limits on consecutive nights, and compensation or time off after night pages.
  • Ownership: services have named owning teams; "you build it, you run it" aligns incentives for quality. Escalation paths (secondary, team lead, incident commander, vendor) are defined.
  • Onboarding: shadow shifts, game days and "wheel of misfortune" role plays, access prepared in advance.

Preventing alert fatigue

  • Page only on symptoms that need human action now: SLO burn rates, user-facing error ratio or latency, not resource noise. Use multi-window multi-burn-rate alerts (for example 2 percent of monthly budget in 1 hour) so fast burns page and slow burns create tickets.
  • Every page must be actionable, urgent and linked to a runbook with context, dashboards and recent changes. If an alert needs no action, delete or demote it.
  • Group, deduplicate and inhibit (Alertmanager), use for: durations, and route low-severity to chat or tickets.
  • Weekly alert review: list top noisy alerts, pages outside working hours, time to acknowledge, false positive rate; assign owners to fix or remove. Track pages per shift, night pages, time to resolve and toil percentage.
  • Automate frequent fixes (self-healing, auto-scaling, restarts with limits), and reduce incident causes through reliability work.

Process around incidents

Clear severity levels, incident commander role for major incidents, blameless postmortems with action items tracked to completion, and error budget policy: when the budget is burned, prioritise reliability work over features, which reduces future pages. Share incident load fairly and rotate the incident commander role.

Wellbeing

Respect rest after heavy nights, avoid on-call for people recovering from burnout, offer voluntary opt-outs when feasible, allow no-meeting mornings after incidents, and treat on-call health as a leadership metric. Survey the team regularly.

Pitfalls

Tiny rotations (3 people), heroes who absorb all pages, alerts without owners, noisy test environments paging production teams, and measuring only uptime while ignoring human cost.

Likely follow-ups

  • What size and shape should the rotation be and when do you need follow-the-sun?
  • What metrics show alert fatigue and how do you reduce it?
  • How do postmortems and error budgets feed back into on-call load?
