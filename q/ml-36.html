Why is the dot-product scaled by $\sqrt{d_k}$ in the scaled dot-product attention formula?

ML / AI engineer · Gear 3 (Senior) · Transformers · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

If the query and key entries have zero mean and unit variance, their dot product over d_k dimensions has variance d_k, so its typical size grows like sqrt(d_k). Large logits push the softmax toward a one-hot output with tiny gradients. Dividing by sqrt(d_k) brings the variance back to 1 and keeps training stable.

Explained in depth

The argument

Let q and k have independent components with mean 0 and variance 1. The score is q . k = sum over i of q_i * k_i. Each term has mean 0 and variance 1, and the d_k terms are independent, so the sum has mean 0 and variance d_k (standard deviation sqrt(d_k)).

With d_k = 64 the scores have a standard deviation of 8. Differences between logits of 10 or more are common, and a softmax then puts almost all of its weight on one key.

Why that hurts

The softmax gradient for each weight is proportional to p(1 - p). When the output is nearly one-hot, p is close to 0 or 1, so the gradients are tiny. Learning stalls, and the model effectively picks one token early on. Scaling by 1 / sqrt(d_k) gives the logits unit variance whatever the head size, so the softmax stays in a range where gradients flow.

softmax( Q K^T / sqrt(d_k) ) V

Caveats

  • The derivation assumes independent, zero-mean, unit-variance entries, which holds roughly at initialisation and only approximately during training.
  • The original transformer paper noted that without scaling, dot-product attention performs worse than additive attention for larger d_k.
  • Some recent models add QK normalisation or learn a temperature instead, and low precision training makes large logits even more risky.

Likely follow-ups

  • What would happen to training if you scaled by d_k instead of its square root?
  • How do QK-normalisation approaches relate to this scaling?
