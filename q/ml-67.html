How does K-Means clustering find centroids, and how do you pick K using the Elbow method and Silhouette score?

ML / AI engineer · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Unsupervised · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

K-Means alternates between assigning each point to its nearest centroid and recomputing each centroid as the mean of its points, converging to a local minimum of within-cluster sum of squares. Choose K with the elbow method (where inertia stops dropping sharply) and the silhouette score (cohesion versus separation, higher is better), ideally combined with domain knowledge and stability checks.

Explained in depth

Objective

Partition n points into K clusters minimising the inertia (within-cluster sum of squared distances): J = Σ_k Σ_{x ∈ C_k} ‖x − μ_k‖²

Lloyd's algorithm

  1. Initialise K centroids (random points or K-Means++).
  2. Assignment step: assign each point to the closest centroid (Euclidean distance).
  3. Update step: set each centroid to the mean of the points assigned to it (the mean minimises the sum of squared distances, which is why centroids are means).
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until assignments stop changing or inertia improvement is below tolerance.

Each step cannot increase J, so the algorithm converges, but only to a local minimum that depends on initialisation. Complexity about O(n · K · d · iterations).

Initialisation

  • Random initialisation can produce poor solutions (two centroids in one cluster, none in another).
  • K-Means++: choose the first centroid uniformly, then pick each next centroid with probability proportional to its squared distance to the nearest existing centroid, spreading centroids out. Gives an O(log K)-competitive approximation and is the default in scikit-learn.
  • Run multiple restarts (n_init) and keep the lowest inertia.
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
for k in range(2, 11):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, init="k-means++", n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(Xs)
    print(k, km.inertia_, silhouette_score(Xs, km.labels_))

Choosing K

  1. Elbow method: plot inertia versus K. Inertia always decreases as K grows (K = n gives 0), so look for the "elbow" where the improvement flattens. Ambiguous when the curve is smooth; use the kneedle algorithm to automate.
  2. Silhouette score: for each point, s = (b − a) / max(a, b), where a is the mean distance to points in its own cluster and b is the mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Range −1 to 1: near 1 well clustered, near 0 on the boundary, negative likely misassigned. Average silhouette across points peaks at a good K. Silhouette plots show per-cluster quality and size imbalance. Computing it is O(n²), so subsample for big data.
  3. Other criteria: gap statistic (compares inertia with a null reference distribution), Davies-Bouldin (lower is better), Calinski-Harabasz (higher is better), BIC for Gaussian mixtures.
  4. Stability: re-cluster on bootstrap samples, check agreement (adjusted Rand index).
  5. Business usefulness: the number of segments marketing can act on, interpretability, and downstream performance.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Clusters are convex and roughly spherical, similar variance and size (since it uses Euclidean distance to a mean). Elongated, nested or non-convex clusters (rings) fail; use DBSCAN, spectral clustering or Gaussian mixtures.
  • Sensitive to feature scaling (standardise), outliers (means move; use K-Medoids or trimming), and irrelevant features (use PCA or feature selection).
  • Must specify K; hard assignments only; poor in very high dimensions (distance concentration), where dimension reduction helps.
  • Categorical data: use K-Modes or K-Prototypes.

Scale

Mini-batch K-Means updates centroids from small random batches, trading a little accuracy for large speed and streaming ability; implementations in Spark MLlib and FAISS (GPU) cluster billions of vectors.

Pitfalls

Not scaling features, trusting a single random initialisation, choosing K only by inertia, interpreting clusters without profiling them, and applying K-Means to data with mixed types.
