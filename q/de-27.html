Compare Snappy, Zstandard (Zstd), and Gzip compression algorithms in data engineering.

Data engineer · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Storage · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Gzip gives high compression but is slow and not splittable on its own. Snappy compresses and decompresses very fast with a modest ratio, which suits hot analytics data. Zstandard offers a tunable range, usually better ratios than Snappy and faster than Gzip, and is increasingly the default. Choose by the balance of storage cost, CPU time and read speed.

Splittability

A compressed file is splittable if a reader can start in the middle and decompress an independent block, so many tasks can process one file in parallel. A plain .gz file is not splittable, so one task must read the entire file. Columnar formats such as Parquet and ORC compress internally per page or stripe, so they stay splittable with any codec. Container formats compress blocks, so files are splittable too.

Choosing

  • Frequently queried data: favour speed, so Snappy or low-level Zstd. CPU is often the bottleneck for scans.
  • Cold or archival data, large text logs: higher Zstd levels or Gzip to save storage and network.
  • Network-bound pipelines (cross-region replication, Kafka): Zstd often reduces bytes enough to pay for its CPU.
  • Measure with your own data, because ratios depend on content (repeated strings compress well, encrypted or already-compressed data does not).

Notes

Zstd supports trained dictionaries for many small records. Take care that all consumers of a topic or file support the codec.
