What is the difference between mentoring and sponsoring, and how do you sponsor underrepresented engineers?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · People · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Mentoring is advice and skill development given to someone you coach, while sponsoring is using your influence and reputation to open doors: recommending them for stretch projects, promotions and visibility. Underrepresented engineers are often over-mentored and under-sponsored, so effective leaders pair feedback with advocacy, assign high-visibility work, and track equitable outcomes.

Explained in depth

Definitions

MentoringSponsoring
Relationshipguidance, feedback, listening; mentor need not be senior or in the same orgadvocacy by someone with power and influence
Actionshare advice, teach skills, help with navigation and confidencerecommend for projects, stretch roles, promotions, speaking, hires; put their reputation on the line
Risk for the giverlowhigher: the sponsor's credibility is tied to the protégé's performance
Outcomeskills and confidenceaccess and advancement

Research (Center for Talent Innovation) shows that sponsorship drives promotion and pay outcomes, and that women and people from underrepresented groups often have mentors but fewer sponsors, which contributes to the "glass ceiling".

How to sponsor effectively

  1. Identify talent through evidence, not affinity: review work output, design docs, incident handling, mentoring and impact data, and look beyond the people who are already visible or who resemble you. Ask peers and tech leads for names, and examine whose work gets noticed.
  2. Open doors with specific actions:
  • Recommend them for high-visibility, high-risk projects, technical lead roles on launches, architecture reviews, on-call leadership, customer escalations, conference talks and cross-team initiatives.
  • Speak up in rooms they are not in: in calibration and promotion meetings, staffing and reorg discussions, say "I have seen X lead Y, with these results, and I think they are ready for Z". Prepare the promotion packet and evidence with them.
  • Give credit publicly (naming them in updates and demos, tagging in design docs), and correct misattribution and interruptions in meetings ("I would like to hear Priya finish").
  • Introduce them to senior leaders and relevant networks, invite them to skip-level conversations.
  • Provide air cover if a stretch assignment goes wrong, and support them through the learning.
  • Advocate for fair compensation and level, checking for gaps against peers.
  1. Pair with mentoring and feedback: sponsorship requires candour. Share specific, actionable feedback, define what "ready" looks like, and set a development plan. Sponsors should be confident the person can perform, so give them the stretch with preparation.
  2. Make it a system, not a favour: create a formal sponsorship programme where senior leaders each sponsor one or two high-potential people from underrepresented groups, with goals, check-ins and accountability. Publish criteria for staffing high-visibility work and make opportunities visible and open (internal job boards for projects, rotating tech lead roles) so access does not depend only on relationships. Avoid concentrating all sponsorship on people similar to the sponsor.
  3. Address the system: review promotion rates, time at level, rating distributions, project assignments (glamour vs non-promotable work), and compensation by group. Rebalance non-promotable work (note-taking, party planning, onboarding) fairly. Inspect interview and calibration processes for bias, and ensure sponsors on committees are trained.
  4. Respect autonomy: ask what the person wants (their goals, whether they want a leadership path), do not assume, and avoid tokenism or putting someone in a spotlight without support.

Example

"I noticed a backend engineer who consistently fixed hard reliability issues but rarely presented. I asked what she wanted, and she said staff-level scope. I proposed her to lead the migration of our queue infrastructure, wrote the case for the staffing, arranged for her to present at the architecture review and the all-hands, and gave feedback on her design doc. During calibration I presented the evidence of her cross-team impact. She was promoted at the next cycle."

Measuring success

Track outcome metrics: share of underrepresented engineers in lead and staff roles, promotion velocity, retention and engagement, pay equity, representation in high-visibility projects, and survey data on belonging. Review sponsor programme participation and results annually.

Pitfalls

Calling mentoring "sponsorship", sponsoring only people who remind you of yourself, tokenism, neglecting to give honest feedback, taking credit for protégé success, and failing to fix structural barriers.
