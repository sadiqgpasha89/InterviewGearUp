How do you manage up effectively to keep your Engineering Director or VP aligned and informed?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Management · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Manage up by understanding your director's priorities and communication style, sharing context proactively with concise updates on progress, risks and decisions needed, bringing options with a recommendation instead of problems alone, surfacing bad news early, aligning on expectations and success measures, and building trust through reliability so you gain autonomy and support.

Explained in depth

Why it matters

Your director or VP controls priorities, resources, and your team's visibility. If they are surprised, misinformed or lacking context, they cannot advocate for you or make good decisions. Managing up is not politics or flattery; it is making their job easier by giving them what they need to succeed, and it benefits your team.

Understand them

  • Goals and pressures: their OKRs, what their boss asks of them, budget constraints, company strategy, and what keeps them up at night (reliability, hiring, cost, customer escalations).
  • Style: do they prefer short written summaries or conversations, data or narrative, frequent or occasional updates, early drafts or polished proposals, decisive or consensus-driven? Adapt your format. Ask directly: "How do you like to be kept informed? What would make you feel confident about my area?"
  • Expectations: agree on what success looks like for you and the team for the quarter, decision rights (what you can decide without asking), and how you communicate urgent issues.

Communicate proactively

  1. Regular one-to-ones: own the agenda. Share a short pre-read: top priorities, progress on goals, risks and blockers, decisions or help needed, people topics (hiring, performance, morale), and wins to recognise. Use a living doc across meetings.
  2. Weekly written update (one page or a short message): status (green, amber, red) with reasons, key accomplishments, next steps, risks with mitigations, asks. Make it skimmable; lead with the bottom line. Share metrics that matter (delivery vs plan, reliability, hiring, team health).
  3. No surprises: tell them early about slips, incidents, attrition risks, security issues, customer escalations, and conflicts with other teams. Give the facts, impact, what you are doing about it, options and your recommendation, and what you need from them. Early bad news with a plan builds trust; late bad news destroys it.
  4. Bring solutions, not just problems: for a decision, present two or three options with trade-offs (cost, risk, timeline), a recommendation, and what happens if no decision is made. Make it easy to say yes.
  5. Frame in business terms: connect technical work to customer impact, revenue, risk and cost ("this migration reduces checkout failures by 30 percent and avoids a $400k licence renewal").
  6. Make your boss look good and informed: give them material to represent your team upward (a few talking points, achievements, a one-slide summary), credit them appropriately, and equip them with answers to likely questions from their peers.
  7. Align early on important decisions: pre-wire proposals in one-to-ones before the larger meeting, understand objections, and use data.
  8. Ask for feedback and sponsorship: "What should I do more or less of? Where do you see gaps in my area?" Request visibility and stretch assignments, and advocate for your team's needs (headcount, tooling, tech debt) with clear business cases.

Disagreeing well

Ask questions to understand the reasoning, present your view privately with evidence and risks, propose alternatives, and confirm the decision. If overruled, disagree and commit: support the decision fully with your team, and track outcomes factually. Escalate only for ethical, legal, safety or severe business risk issues, in line with policy.

Manage your own reliability

Deliver what you promised, communicate when plans change, be accurate with estimates, and follow through. Reliability earns autonomy.

Pitfalls

Updating only when asked, hiding problems until fixed, over-polished decks with no decisions, venting complaints without options, going around the boss without telling them, and assuming they know the context.
