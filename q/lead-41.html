How do you manage and maintain culture across distributed, remote, and asynchronous engineering teams?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Organization · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Sustain culture in distributed teams by making it explicit and written: define values and working agreements, default to asynchronous written communication with clear decision records, create deliberate connection through rituals and occasional in-person time, ensure equal access to information and opportunity regardless of location, and measure belonging with surveys and one-to-ones.

Explained in depth

Why it needs deliberate design

In an office, culture spreads through proximity: overheard conversations, lunches, observing leaders. Distributed teams lose that implicit channel, so culture must be written down, practised and reinforced or it fragments into a set of individual habits. Risks: isolation, time-zone inequity, proximity bias (people seen more get promoted), communication overload or silence, burnout from always-on norms, and decisions made in private chats.

Principles and practices

  1. Make values and norms explicit: a short team charter / working agreement: core values, how we make decisions, how we communicate (channels and expected response times), meeting norms, working hours and overlap windows, how to disagree, how to ask for help, on-call expectations. Review it quarterly and during onboarding.
  2. Async-first, written communication: default to docs, RFCs, recorded demos (Loom), threads and clear status updates; use meetings for discussion, relationship, and complex decisions. Document decisions in a searchable place (handbook, wiki, ADRs), so people in other time zones are not blocked or excluded. GitLab's public handbook is a model. Use clear writing norms: context, ask, deadline.
  3. Equal access and visibility: remote-first behaviours even when some people are in an office: meetings with everyone on their own device, no side conversations that exclude remote people, chat for follow-up, shared agenda and notes, rotate meeting times across time zones, record sessions.
  4. Connection and trust: scheduled one-to-ones (weekly or biweekly) focused on the person, not only status; team rituals such as virtual coffee pairings (Donut), show-and-tell, retrospectives, social channels, games, learning clubs; onboarding buddy and a 30-60-90 plan; periodic in-person offsites (once or twice a year) for relationship building and strategy; recognition of achievements (peer shoutouts, kudos channel).
  5. Psychological safety: encourage questions, normalise cameras optional, ask for feedback, model vulnerability, run blameless postmortems, give feedback privately and promptly, and watch for people who stay silent.
  6. Outcome-focused management: set clear goals (OKRs), measure results rather than hours online, give autonomy, and avoid surveillance tools. Provide clear career paths and promotion criteria that do not favour those who are visible in an office; ensure sponsorship for remote members.
  7. Healthy boundaries: core overlap hours, no expectation of immediate replies outside working hours, scheduled-send for messages, holidays honoured across countries, encourage time off, watch for after-hours activity as a burnout signal.
  8. Tooling: Slack or Teams with channel conventions, Notion or Confluence, GitHub and Jira, Zoom or Meet, Figma or Miro, a handbook that new hires read. Fewer, well-chosen tools.
  9. Inclusion across geography: consider language and cultural differences, time-zone fairness in meetings and on-call (follow the sun), local holidays and benefits, equipment and home-office stipends, and mental health support.

Measuring culture

Quarterly pulse surveys (belonging, clarity, workload, trust in leadership, manager quality), eNPS, retention and attrition by location, participation metrics in async discussions (not for policing), skip-level conversations, exit interview themes, and retro actions.

Leader behaviours

Leaders over-communicate priorities and context, say the unwritten rules aloud, respond in public channels so others learn, share their own working hours and mistakes, and visibly honour the norms (not messaging at midnight and expecting answers).

Pitfalls

Recreating the office with endless video calls, no documentation so only some know decisions, ignoring time zones, relying on tools to create culture, and treating remote employees as second class.
