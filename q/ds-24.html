How do you approach time series forecasting: stationarity, seasonality, model choice and backtesting?

Data scientist · Gear 2 (Mid-level) · Time series forecasting · Difficulty: medium · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Plot and decompose the series into trend, seasonality and noise, handle stationarity through differencing or transformations if the model needs it, start with simple baselines such as seasonal naive, compare classical models like ETS and ARIMA with machine learning on lag features, and judge them with rolling-origin backtests on a horizon that matches the business need.

Explained in depth

1. Understand the series

  • Plot raw data and zoom into recent periods; check frequency, gaps, outliers, level shifts (price changes, outages), and calendar effects (holidays, promotions).
  • Decompose with STL or classical decomposition into trend, seasonal (possibly multiple: daily, weekly, yearly) and residual. Check additive versus multiplicative (seasonal amplitude growing with level suggests multiplicative or a log transform).
  • Autocorrelation (ACF) and partial autocorrelation (PACF) reveal dependence structure.
  • Define the forecast horizon, granularity and decision: weekly demand for 12 weeks for inventory is not the same as next-hour load.

2. Stationarity

Many classical models (ARIMA) assume a stationary series (stable mean, variance, autocovariance). Check visually and with ADF and KPSS tests. Make stationary by differencing (remove trend), seasonal differencing, or log or Box-Cox transforms (stabilise variance). Machine learning models with engineered features do not strictly require stationarity but still struggle with strong trends and extrapolation.

3. Baselines first

Naive (last value), seasonal naive (value from the same period last cycle), moving average, drift. They are surprisingly hard to beat and give a reference for MASE or skill scores.

Use exogenous variables known in the future (price plans, promotions, holidays, weather forecasts).

5. Evaluation: backtest the way you deploy

Random k-fold leaks the future. Use rolling-origin (walk-forward) cross-validation: train up to time t, forecast the next h periods, move the origin forward, repeat. Compute errors across origins and horizons.

from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit
import numpy as np
errors = []
for tr, te in TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=8, test_size=14).split(df):
    m = fit(df.iloc[tr]); errors.append(np.abs(m.predict(df.iloc[te]) - df.iloc[te]["y"]).mean())

Metrics: MAE, RMSE, MAPE/sMAPE (careful with zeros), MASE (scale-free versus naive), weighted metrics for revenue-weighted importance, and prediction interval coverage and pinball loss for quantiles.

6. Uncertainty and operations

Provide prediction intervals (model-based, bootstrap, conformal prediction, quantile regression) because decisions (safety stock) depend on the distribution, not the point. Monitor forecast error and bias in production, retrain on a schedule or on drift, handle structural breaks (COVID, pricing changes), and intermittent demand with Croston-type methods or count models.

Pitfalls

Evaluating with a random split, ignoring holidays and promotions, forecasting with features not known at forecast time, optimising MAPE on low-volume items, and over-trusting a model without a seasonal naive benchmark.
