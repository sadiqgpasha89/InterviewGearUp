How do you manage cross-team dependencies when an initiative requires alignment across multiple platform teams?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Delivery · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Manage cross-team dependencies by making them explicit and early: map dependencies during planning, agree outcomes, owners and dates in a shared plan, align on interfaces and contracts so teams can work in parallel, hold a lightweight regular sync, escalate blockers quickly with options, and give the initiative a single accountable owner with executive sponsorship.

Explained in depth

The problem

Platform and product teams have separate priorities, roadmaps and incentives. Common failures: late discovery of needs, unclear ownership, mismatched assumptions about interfaces, waiting for capacity, and last-minute integration. Dependencies cause most schedule slips in multi-team programmes.

Approach

  1. Establish a single accountable owner and sponsor: one person (programme lead, tech lead or EM) is accountable for the end-to-end outcome. An executive sponsor helps resolve priority conflicts between teams. Define a short charter: goal, success metrics, scope, teams, decision rights, and timeline.
  2. Map dependencies early: in kickoff, each team lists what it needs from others and what it provides, with owner, date, and acceptance criteria. Visualise as a dependency matrix or board. Repeat in each planning cycle. Frameworks: SAFe PI Planning (a programme board with dependency strings), Big Room Planning, quarterly planning with a dependency review, Team Topologies interaction modes.
  3. Agree on outcomes, not tasks: make a written working agreement or service contract: what will be delivered, quality expectations, API or interface spec, SLAs for support, capacity committed (for example "two engineers for six weeks"), and definition of done. If a team cannot commit, discover it now and re-scope.
  4. Decouple through interfaces: define API contracts, schemas and mocks first (OpenAPI, protobuf, consumer-driven contract tests), so the consumer can build against a stub while the provider implements. Use feature flags, versioned APIs, and backward-compatible changes. Integrate continuously (early end-to-end slice) not at the end.
  5. Create cadence and visibility: a short weekly or biweekly sync of leads (15 to 30 minutes) focused on blockers, status of dependencies (red, amber, green), and decisions needed; a shared dashboard (Jira dependency links, Linear projects, Notion tracker); a public channel for the initiative; demos at milestones to show progress and surface integration issues.
  6. Escalate effectively: when a dependency slips, communicate early with impact, options (reduce scope, change sequence, borrow capacity, accept delay), and a recommendation. Go first to the other team's lead, then to the shared manager or sponsor. Keep it factual, not blaming.
  7. Build relationships and reciprocity: understand the other team's goals, constraints and incentives; offer help (contribute code or review, share a test environment, provide clear requirements), thank them publicly, and align on "what is in it for them" (platform adoption, reduced support, shared metrics).
  8. Prioritisation alignment: tie work to company-level goals (OKRs) so teams see shared value. Leaders from each team agree on relative priority, and conflicts go to a prioritisation forum or product council.

Reduce dependencies structurally

  • Team boundaries aligned to value streams (stream-aligned teams with enabling and platform teams per Team Topologies).
  • Self-service platforms, internal APIs with documentation, InnerSource (allow other teams to contribute changes via pull requests with clear guidelines), shared libraries owned and versioned.
  • Avoid shared databases and tightly coupled deployments; use event-driven integration and independently deployable services.
  • Pairing or temporarily embedding an engineer in the dependency's team for critical work.

Metrics and review

Track blocked time, number of cross-team handoffs, lead time, percentage of dependencies delivered on time, and survey satisfaction between teams. Hold a retrospective across teams after milestones.

Pitfalls

Assuming a verbal "yes" is a commitment, discovering dependencies in the final sprint, waiting passively for status, escalating without options, unclear ownership, and designing org structures that guarantee constant dependencies.
