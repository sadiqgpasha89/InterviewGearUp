How do you manage unrealistic sales commitments made to enterprise clients without engineering review?

Tech lead / Engineering manager · Gear 3 (Senior) · Communication · Difficulty: hard · AI-drafted, not yet reviewed by a person

In 30 seconds

Handle unrealistic sales commitments by acting fast and calmly: find out exactly what was promised and to whom, assess feasibility and cost with engineering facts, bring the account and product leaders into one conversation, offer options such as scope trade-offs, phased delivery or a revised date, and put a pre-sales technical review in place so commitments are checked before they are made.

Explained in depth

Understand before reacting

When a salesperson promises a feature, integration, SLA or date to an enterprise client without engineering review, the instinct is frustration, but the first job is facts: What exactly was promised (written in a contract, RFP response, email, or verbal)? To whom? Is it binding (contract clause, SOW) or an informal expectation? What is the deal value, strategic importance, deadline and alternatives? What does the customer really need (the outcome) versus what was said? Talk with the account executive and sales engineer without blame, then check with legal about contractual commitments.

Assess feasibility with engineering

Quickly analyse the requested work: scope, dependencies, risks, effort ranges (not a single number), impact on the roadmap and other commitments, security and compliance implications, and operational costs. Use a spike if uncertainty is high. Provide a one-page assessment: what can be delivered by when with confidence levels, what is infeasible and why, and what the opportunity cost is (what would be displaced).

Bring the right people together

Convene product, sales, customer success, engineering and (if needed) legal and executive sponsors. Present facts and options, not complaints. Acknowledge the sales team's objective (winning the deal) while being clear on constraints. Align on priorities and trade-offs: revenue value versus cost, strategic fit, and risk to existing customers.

Options to offer

  1. Deliver as promised if feasible and valuable, with an agreed funding of capacity: stop or defer other work (explicitly chosen by product or executive leadership), temporary resources, or contractor help.
  2. Reduce scope to a minimum viable version that meets the customer's real need (for example a configurable export now, full integration later), delivered via a pilot or beta.
  3. Phase delivery: milestone dates with early value; use feature flags and customer-specific configuration if appropriate.
  4. Offer workarounds: professional services, manual processes, third-party tools or a partner integration until the full feature is available.
  5. Renegotiate the date with a credible plan, transparent communication, and incentives or concessions if needed (pricing adjustments, extended trial, dedicated support).
  6. Decline if the commitment is harmful or misaligned (custom work that fragments the product, or security risk), with leaders presenting the rationale and alternative solutions.

Communicate with the customer

Prefer a joint call led by the account executive with product or engineering leadership present. Be honest, concise and solution-oriented: acknowledge the expectation, explain the constraint without blaming the internal team, propose the plan and what you need from them (requirements, pilot participation, feedback). Customers often respond well to transparency and a credible roadmap more than to a missed promise. Document agreements in writing.

Protect the team

Do not silently absorb the commitment through crunch. If leadership decides to proceed, ensure explicit trade-offs (scope or timeline for other work), realistic planning, quality standards and staffing, and recognition. Insist on definition of done (security, tests, observability). Keep engineers informed of the "why" and customer context, so they are motivated, and shield them from direct pressure.

Fix the system

  • Pre-sales technical review: sales engineers or architects review non-standard commitments, RFP answers, custom requests and SLAs before they are made; a deal desk or approval workflow requiring engineering and product sign-off for non-roadmap commitments, with a checklist (security, compliance, SLA, integrations).
  • Clear product boundaries and a shared roadmap: sales training on what is available, what is planned (with confidence levels), what is not, and how to talk about roadmap without making promises ("forward-looking statements" guidelines). Provide a capabilities and limits document, and a fast channel to ask engineering questions.
  • Contract and policy: standard terms limiting roadmap commitments, legal review of special clauses, and tracking of all commitments in a registry visible to product and engineering.
  • Capacity for strategic deals: a defined allowance (for example a percentage of capacity or a solutions engineering team) to handle custom requests, and an intake process with business-case scoring.
  • Relationships and incentives: regular syncs between sales, product and engineering, shared forecasts, and metrics that reward realistic deals (customer satisfaction, renewals), not only bookings.
  • Retrospective on the case: what happened, how to prevent it, without blame.

Pitfalls

Public blame, refusing flatly without options, silently building under crunch, letting the commitment drive the roadmap unnoticed, and no process change afterwards.
